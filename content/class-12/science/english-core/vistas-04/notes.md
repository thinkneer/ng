# The Enemy — Pearl S. Buck

Text: NCERT *Vistas*, Chapter 4 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)). Pearl S. Buck (1892–1973), an American Nobel laureate who grew up in China, sets this story in **Japan during the Second World War**. A Japanese surgeon finds a wounded **American prisoner of war** on his beach. Everything in the story flows from one question: **when a man's duty as a doctor and his duty to his country point in opposite directions, which does he follow?** It is the longest Vistas chapter and one of the most-asked for the 5-mark question.

## Dr Sadao Hoki and Hana

**Dr Sadao Hoki** is a Japanese surgeon and scientist whose house stands on a rocky spot on the **Japanese coast**, where as a boy he played among the pines. His **father** had one ambition for him — his education — and sent him at twenty-two to **America** to study surgery and medicine. Sadao returned at thirty, a famous surgeon, perfecting a discovery that would render wounds entirely clean. He has not been sent to the front with the troops for two reasons: the **old General** may need an operation, and Sadao's research is valuable.

He met **Hana**, his wife, in America, at a professor's house; he waited to fall in love with her until he was sure she was Japanese, and the marriage was arranged in the old Japanese way. They have two children and a traditional household: servants, including the old **gardener**, the **cook**, and **Yumi**, the children's nurse.

**In one breath:** Sadao, an American-trained Japanese surgeon kept home to treat the General, lives on the coast with his wife Hana, two children and old-fashioned servants.

## The man on the beach

One misty evening Sadao and Hana see a figure stagger out of the sea and fall on the sand. They run down and find a **white man**, badly **wounded**: a gunshot wound on his back, reopened by the rocks, bleeding; a battered face; **blond hair**. His torn cap carries the letters **U.S. Navy** — an American **prisoner of war**, escaped, washed ashore on their beach.

Here the dilemma begins. If they shelter him they are **traitors**; if they turn him in he will **die**; if they push him back into the sea he will die faster. Sadao's training speaks first: he **stops the bleeding** with sea moss and remarks, almost without thinking, that the wound needs an operation. Hana asks the question the story turns on — what will they do? They agree that the best thing would be to put him back in the sea; but neither moves. Finally Sadao decides: he will **take the man into the house**, operate, and **hand him over to the police** when he recovers. They carry him in to Sadao's father's room — the room where a white man had never been.

:::tip The dilemma in one line
Duty as a doctor (save a life) against duty as a Japanese citizen (hand over or kill the enemy). The whole story — his hesitation, the servants' protest, the General's plan, the escape — is Sadao working through this.
:::

**In one breath:** a wounded American POW washes up on the beach; the surgeon's instinct to save and the citizen's duty to report collide, and Sadao carries the man indoors.

## The servants refuse

The servants are terrified and angry. **Yumi** refuses to wash the white man; Hana, trembling, washes him herself. The **gardener** says the master should not heal the wound of a white man — the sea was ready to take him, and if the master heals what the gun and the sea did, they will take revenge on all of them. The **cook** says the master is so proud of his skill that he cannot help himself. The servants represent the ordinary Japanese view: this is **the enemy**, and loyalty to the nation is simple. Within a week they **leave the house** in a body, loyal to the family in their way (they do not inform) but unwilling to share its guilt.

**In one breath:** the servants refuse to help an enemy and finally leave; their fear and prejudice show how dangerous Sadao's choice is.

## The operation

Sadao operates with Hana as his assistant; she has never seen an operation and runs out to vomit, then returns. He finds the **bullet lodged near the kidney**, cuts carefully, and removes it. Throughout, his two selves speak: the **surgeon** mutters to the unconscious man about the pain and the danger; the **Japanese** in him thinks it would be simplest to let the man die — and yet his hands work to save him. When the man wakes he is terrified; Sadao tells him curtly not to be afraid. Over the following days the young man — **Tom**, barely more than a boy — recovers, and Sadao finds himself irritated by his own kindness and unable to explain it.

Sadao also sends a **message to the police chief** — and then does not. He writes a letter and keeps it in his drawer. The delay is the measure of his conflict.

**In one breath:** Sadao removes the bullet near the kidney and nurses the prisoner back to health, hating his own humanity, and never sends the letter to the police.

## The General's promise

The **old General**, whom Sadao attends, falls ill, and Sadao tells him everything about the prisoner. The General is a man of total **self-interest**: he worries only that Sadao must not be arrested, because he **trusts no other surgeon** to operate on him. He promises to send his private **assassins** to kill the prisoner quietly in the night and remove the body; Sadao need only leave the outer partition open. The General even suggests it would be helpful if the doctor would himself... but Sadao's expression stops him.

For three nights Sadao **lies awake**, listening for the assassins, dreading the discovery of a corpse at dawn. Each morning the prisoner is **still alive** and well. The assassins never come.

**In one breath:** Sadao confesses to the self-centred General, who promises to have the prisoner assassinated; three sleepless nights later, nothing has happened.

## The escape

Unable to bear the wait, Sadao decides to act himself. He tells Tom that he must leave: he will put his **boat** on the shore that night, with **food** and **extra clothing**, and the man is to row to the **little island** off the coast, which is uninhabited and not patrolled, and wait there for a **Korean fishing boat** to pass. Sadao gives him a **flashlight** with instructions: **two flashes** if he is in need of food, **one flash** if he is safe — and none if all is well. Dressed in Japanese clothes with a black cloth over his blond hair, the prisoner leaves. For days Sadao watches the island at sunset: no signal. The young man has gone.

When the General recovers, Sadao reports that the prisoner has escaped and asks about the assassins. The General admits he **forgot** — he was so taken up with his own illness — and asks Sadao not to mention it, because it would look like a dereliction of duty; he praises himself for the one quality he has, **self-interest**. Sadao is safe.

**In one breath:** Sadao sends the prisoner by boat to an empty island to wait for a Korean fishing boat, with a flashlight for signals; the General admits he simply forgot the assassins.

## Why could I not kill him?

The story ends with Sadao at his window, looking at the dark island, remembering Americans he knew: the ignorant **landlady** who nursed him through influenza, the professor and his dull wife, the **prejudice** he met in America when he was refused a room for being Japanese. He thinks of all the white faces, and calls them **repulsive**. And then he thinks of the prisoner's young face and asks himself the question the story has been asking: **why could he not kill him?** He has no answer — and the reader does: because he is, before anything else, a doctor and a human being.

:::tip The ending is a question, not a speech
Do not write that Sadao "realised all men are brothers". The story is subtler: he still feels prejudice, still dislikes Americans, and still saved the man. The humanity is in the action, not in a change of opinion.
:::

**In one breath:** remembering the prejudice he suffered in America, Sadao still cannot explain why he could not kill the prisoner — his humanity acted before his nationalism could.

## Themes, characters and the writer's craft

- **Humanity above nationality:** a doctor's duty to a life outweighs a citizen's duty to his nation's war.
- **Conflict between duty and conscience:** the central dilemma, dramatised in Sadao's delays — the unsent letter, the sleepless nights.
- **War and prejudice:** the servants' hatred, the American landlords' racism, Sadao's own dislike — Buck shows prejudice on both sides.
- **Self-interest vs principle:** the General's selfishness ironically saves both Sadao and the prisoner.
- **Courage of a woman:** Hana overcomes revulsion and fear to wash and nurse the enemy and to stand by her husband.

**Characters:** **Sadao** (brilliant, dutiful, proud, honest about his own conflict, humane in action), **Hana** (loyal, frightened, brave, compassionate), **the General** (self-absorbed, ironic instrument of good), **Tom** (the young prisoner, terrified then grateful), **the servants** (voice of the nation's prejudice).

**Craft:** third-person narration that moves inside Sadao's thoughts; suspense (the beach, the operation, the nights); irony (the General's forgetfulness); an open ending.

**In one breath:** "The Enemy" is a wartime story in which a surgeon's hands save the man his country calls an enemy — and he cannot explain why, which is Buck's whole point.
