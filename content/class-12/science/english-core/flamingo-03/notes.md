# Deep Water — William Douglas

Text: NCERT *Flamingo*, Chapter 3 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)), an excerpt from Douglas's autobiographical book *Of Men and Mountains*. William O. Douglas was an American judge, and this is a true account of how a childhood terror of water nearly ruled his life, and how he beat it. The chapter is a straight line: **fear is planted → fear grows → fear is faced step by step → fear is tested and conquered → what it taught him.** Keep that line in your head and every question becomes easy.

## Where the fear began

Douglas's aversion to water started when he was **three or four**. His father took him to a **beach in California**; the waves knocked him down and swept over him, he was buried in water, his breath was gone and he was frightened. His father laughed, but the child never forgot the overpowering force of the waves. The fear was small then — a seed.

At **ten or eleven** he decided to learn to swim at the **YMCA pool in Yakima**, Washington, where he lived. The pool was safe — only two or three feet deep at the shallow end and about nine feet at the deep end, with a gradual drop. The **Yakima River** was treacherous and his mother kept warning him about drownings there, so the pool seemed the sensible choice. He got water wings and paddled in the shallow end, slowly gaining confidence, though the old unpleasant memory stirred.

**In one breath:** a beach wave at age three planted the fear; at ten he began at the safe YMCA pool, aversion and all.

## The misadventure at the pool

One day he reached the pool when nobody else was there. Timid about going in alone, he sat on the side waiting. A big boy — about **eighteen**, a bruiser with muscles — came along, called him "Skinny", asked how he would like to be ducked, and before Douglas could answer **picked him up and tossed him into the deep end**. Douglas landed sitting, swallowed water and went straight to the bottom.

What follows is the most-asked passage: the account of **near-drowning**. On the way down he planned coolly: when his feet hit the bottom he would make a big jump, come to the surface, lie flat and paddle to the edge. The nine feet seemed like ninety. His lungs were ready to burst. When he hit the bottom he jumped with all his might — but came up slowly; he opened his eyes and saw only water with a yellowish glow; he grabbed at a rope but found nothing; he was suffocating, he tried to yell but no sound came. He went down a **second time**, planned again, jumped again — and again found only water, legs paralysed, lungs aching, head throbbing. He was sinking the **third time** when all effort ceased; he relaxed, felt no fear, and lost consciousness. He later described it as a peaceful drowsiness, as if he were being carried off to sleep.

He came to lying on his stomach beside the pool, vomiting; the boy who had thrown him in said he was only fooling and someone said the kid nearly died. He walked home shaking, cried that night and could not eat for days.

:::tip Three attempts, not two
The examiner checks the sequence: first jump (plan, water, grab for the rope), second jump (paralysed legs, no scream), third sinking (all effort stops, peace, darkness). Students who collapse this into one dive lose the content mark.
:::

## The handicap that stayed for years

The physical rescue was not the end. A **haunting fear** took hold: whenever he went near water, the terror returned — his legs went paralysed, icy horror grabbed his heart. It ruined his fishing trips and kept him away from canoeing, boating and swimming. He loved the **Cascades**, the **Tieton** and the **Bumping River**, Warm Lake, the Columbia — and he could not enjoy any of them. Years passed; the fear was a handicap he carried everywhere.

**In one breath:** the terror lasted for years and robbed him of every pleasure connected with water.

## Deciding to fight: the instructor

Finally he decided to get an **instructor** and learn to swim. He went to a pool and practised **five days a week, an hour each day**. The method was slow and systematic — remember its steps:

1. The instructor put a **belt** around Douglas, attached a **rope** to it, and the rope passed through a **pulley** on an overhead cable. Holding the rope, he let Douglas go back and forth across the pool; each trip a piece of the old terror returned, but over **three months** the tension began to slacken.
2. He taught him to put his **face under water and exhale**, and to raise his nose and inhale — repeated hundreds of times, until the panic about water on the face began to fade.
3. He held him at the side of the pool and had him **kick with his legs**, week after week, until the legs that had gone paralysed in the pool relaxed and obeyed.
4. He combined the pieces — breathing, kicking, arm strokes — into a swimmer. In **April**, having started in **October**, the instructor said he was done: he could swim across the pool.

## Testing the fear alone

Douglas was not satisfied: the instructor had taught him to swim, but had the **terror** gone? When he was alone in the pool and a vestige of fear returned, he spoke to it — asking what it could do to him — and swam another length. Then he tested himself in real water: he went to **Lake Wentworth** in New Hampshire, dived off a dock at **Triggs Island** and swam **two miles** across the lake to Stamp Act Island. Only once, in the middle, did the old sensation return; he laughed at it and kept going.

Still not done, he went to the **Warm Lake** in the mountains, camped at **Conrad Meadows**, hiked up the Conrad Creek trail to **Meade Glacier**, and **swam across Warm Lake and back**. Then he shouted with joy — the fear had finally been conquered.

## What the experience taught him

Douglas ends by explaining what the whole ordeal meant. In the face of death he had felt both **terror and peace**, so death itself did not frighten him: only **the fear of death** did. Having conquered that fear, he felt free. He quotes Roosevelt's idea that the only thing to fear is fear itself, and adds his own: the sensation of dying and the terror it produces — that is what fear is, and one who has faced it is released to live fully, to live intensely.

:::tip The message is about fear, not swimming
Long answers should move from the personal story to the universal lesson: fear is a mental enemy, it can be fought with persistence and method, and conquering it gives a larger freedom in life. Mention the "fear of death, not death" idea explicitly.
:::

## Themes, style and the writer's message

- **Fear and its conquest.** Fear is planted by experience, fed by memory, and beaten only by facing it deliberately, bit by bit.
- **Persistence and method.** Six months of daily practice, step by step; no shortcuts.
- **Determination to test oneself.** Swimming in pools was not enough — he sought the lakes to be sure.
- **Psychology of trauma.** The chapter is honest about how long a childhood terror can last and how physical it feels (paralysed legs, icy heart).
- **Autobiographical style.** First person, precise detail (the nine feet, the yellowish water, the pulley), inner monologue during the drowning, simple sentences that build suspense, and a reflective ending.

Characters are few: **Douglas** (narrator), his **father** (the beach), his **mother** (warnings about the Yakima), the **bully** (never named), and the **instructor**, whose patience is the model of good teaching.
