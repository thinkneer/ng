# The case-based passage — reading words and numbers together

Q2 of Section A gives you a **factual passage** of about **300–350 words** — a report, a survey summary, an extract from an article about research — usually with a **table, chart or graph**, and about **eight to ten items** for **10 marks**. Students who are comfortable with the unseen passage sometimes stumble here, because two skills are tested at once: reading **prose** and reading **data**. The method is the same two-pass approach as Q1, with one extra step: **read the visual before the questions**. Confirm the exact format against the 2026–27 sample paper on [cbseacademic.nic.in](https://cbseacademic.nic.in/).

## What a case-based passage contains

A typical Q2 has three layers:

1. **A factual introduction** — what the study, survey or report was about, who did it, and why it matters.
2. **A visual** — a table of figures, a bar or pie chart, or a line graph showing a trend over years.
3. **An interpretation and conclusion** — what the writer makes of the data and what should be done.

The items follow the same three layers: some ask about the **facts** stated (who, what, when), some about the **data** (highest, lowest, difference, trend, percentage), and some ask you to **infer or evaluate** — why a figure might be surprising, what the data suggests, which conclusion the data supports.

**In one breath:** Q2 = factual text + a chart or table + a conclusion; the items test facts, data reading and inference, 10 marks in all.

## Reading the visual first

Before the questions, spend one minute on the **visual**:

- Read the **title** and the **axis labels / column headings** — what is being measured, in what unit (per cent, hours, number of people), for what group and period.
- Find the **largest** and **smallest** values and mark them.
- Note the **trend**: rising, falling, steady, a peak, a sudden change.
- Check any **footnote** ("figures rounded", "sample of 2,000 students").

Most data items can then be answered without rereading the prose: *Which year saw the highest…? By how much did X exceed Y? Which category fell while the others rose?* Do the subtraction or comparison **on the paper**, not in your head; a wrong difference costs the mark even when you have understood everything.

:::tip Units and per cents
If the chart shows percentages, your answer is a percentage, not a number of people; if it shows hours, say hours. "The figure rose by 12" is wrong if the chart is in per cent — write "by 12 percentage points" or "from 48% to 60%".
:::

**In one breath:** read the title, headings, units, extremes and trend of the visual before the questions; do comparisons on paper with the right unit.

## Question types in Q2

- **Fact items (1 mark):** what the survey measured, who conducted it, when, how many respondents. Scan the first paragraph.
- **Data items (1 mark):** the highest/lowest category, a value, a difference, a ranking. Read from the visual; show the figure.
- **Trend items (1 mark):** what rose, fell or stayed steady over the period; the year of the peak.
- **Inference from data (1–2 marks):** what the data suggests (e.g. that a habit is more common among one group); the answer must be something the numbers *support*, not something you believe anyway.
- **Vocabulary (1 mark):** a word from a named paragraph meaning X — same method as Q1.
- **Evaluation / "best supports" MCQ (1 mark):** which statement the passage and data together justify; reject options the data contradicts or does not cover.
- **2-mark item:** two reasons the writer gives, or a conclusion and its supporting figure, in about 40 words.

**In one breath:** facts from the text, numbers from the visual, inferences that the numbers support, one vocabulary item and a 2-mark summary — each with its own place to look.

## Common mistakes

- Reading **a column for a row**: check which way the table runs before copying a value.
- **Mixing up the year** on a line graph: follow the point down to the x-axis with your pencil.
- **Inferring beyond the data**: "students are lazy" is not supported by "students sleep 6 hours"; "students sleep less than the recommended 8 hours" is.
- Giving a **trend** when the item asks for a **value**, or a value when it asks for a trend.
- Forgetting the **2-mark item** needs two points; one point in many words earns one mark.

:::tip Quote the figure
Every data answer should carry the number: "Category B, at 42%, was the highest." An answer without the figure can be marked as a guess.
:::

**In one breath:** check rows vs columns, follow points to the axis, infer only what the numbers support, and always quote the figure.

## Time plan

**12–15 minutes**: 1 minute reading the visual, 1 minute skimming the prose, 1 minute reading the items, 8–10 minutes answering, 1 minute checking arithmetic and units. Do Q2 straight after Q1.

**In one breath:** about 13 minutes, visual first, a minute at the end for arithmetic.

## Practice passage (our own)

The questions on this passage are in **Practice**.

:::skeleton Practice passage — Screen Time and Sleep: A School Survey
(1) In September a group of Class 12 students conducted a survey on screen use and sleep among 400 students of Classes 9 to 12 in their school, 100 from each class. Each student recorded, for one week, the average number of hours spent on phones, laptops and televisions outside school hours, and the average hours of sleep on school nights. The students wanted to find out whether screen time increased with age and whether heavier screen users slept less.

(2) The results are summarised in the table below.

| Class | Average screen time (hours/day) | Average sleep (hours/night) | Students using a screen after 11 p.m. (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9 | 2.4 | 8.1 | 18 |
| 10 | 3.1 | 7.6 | 31 |
| 11 | 4.2 | 6.9 | 52 |
| 12 | 4.6 | 6.4 | 64 |

(3) The pattern was clear in both directions. Average screen time almost doubled from Class 9 to Class 12, while average sleep fell by 1.7 hours over the same span. The sharpest change in both measures came between Classes 10 and 11, the year in which most students receive their own smartphone and begin preparing for competitive examinations. The share of students using a screen after 11 p.m. rose from fewer than one in five in Class 9 to nearly two-thirds in Class 12.

(4) When asked what they did on screens late at night, senior students most often named messaging friends and watching short videos; study-related use accounted for less than a quarter of late-night screen time. Nearly half of the Class 12 respondents said they felt tired in the first two periods of school on most days.

(5) The surveying students concluded that screen use in their school rises with age and that heavier users sleep less, though they noted that a survey cannot prove that one causes the other: older students also carry a heavier study load. They recommended a school campaign on a "screens off by 10:30" habit, and asked that the survey be repeated in March to see whether anything had changed.
:::

**In one breath:** one original factual passage with a four-row table — practise reading the visual first, then the items, in 13 minutes.
