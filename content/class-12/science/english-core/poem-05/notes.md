# Aunt Jennifer's Tigers — Adrienne Rich

Text: NCERT *Flamingo*, Poem 5 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)). Adrienne Rich (1929–2012), an American poet and feminist thinker, wrote this short, three-stanza poem early in her career. On the surface it describes an old woman embroidering tigers on a panel. Underneath, it is about a woman **crushed by marriage and a male-dominated world**, and about **art** as the one place where she can put the courage she herself has never been allowed to show.

## Stanza 1: the tigers on the screen

Aunt Jennifer's tigers **prance** across a **screen** — the cloth panel she is embroidering. They are **bright topaz** (a golden-yellow gem) **denizens** (inhabitants) of a **world of green** (the jungle). They **do not fear the men** standing beneath the tree; they **pace** in **sleek, chivalric certainty**. Every word gives the tigers what the aunt lacks: brightness, freedom, confidence, fearlessness, grace, and a knightly (*chivalric*) nobility. In the aunt's art the tigers are the masters of their world, and the men are simply something they walk past.

:::tip "Chivalric" and "denizens"
Students ask what these words mean. *Denizens* = inhabitants; *chivalric* = like a medieval knight: noble, brave, graceful, honourable. Tigers given a knight's bearing suggest a courage and dignity the aunt admires.
:::

**In one breath:** the embroidered tigers are bright, free, fearless and noble — everything their maker is not.

## Stanza 2: Aunt Jennifer's hands

The second stanza turns from the tigers to the woman who makes them. Her **fingers flutter** through the wool — the movement is nervous and weak, like a trapped bird's wings. She finds even the **ivory needle hard to pull**: a tiny, light needle, yet her fingers struggle with it. Why? Because of the **massive weight of Uncle's wedding band** that **sits heavily** on her hand. A wedding ring is physically light; its weight here is the **burden of marriage** — the demands, fears and submission that her husband and the institution of marriage have loaded onto her for a lifetime. The ring is **Uncle's**, not hers: it marks her as his possession.

:::tip The wedding band is the key symbol
Nearly every extract question on this stanza asks about the wedding band. Answer: it symbolises the oppressive bonds of marriage and male domination; its "massive weight" is the burden of a lifetime of fear and submission that has made even needlework an effort.
:::

**In one breath:** the aunt's fluttering fingers can hardly pull a needle, weighed down by the heavy wedding band — the crushing burden of her marriage.

## Stanza 3: when Aunt is dead

The last stanza looks ahead. When Aunt Jennifer dies, her **terrified hands** will **lie still** — but still **ringed with the ordeals she was mastered by**. Even in death the ring will be on her finger; the trials of her life will have left their mark on her, and she will have been *mastered* (defeated, dominated) by them to the end. Death brings no freedom.

But the **tigers in the panel** she made will **go on prancing**, **proud and unafraid**. The art outlives the artist; the courage she could only imagine survives in her work. There is a double edge: triumph for the tigers, defeat for the aunt. Rich lets both stand.

**In one breath:** death will not free Aunt Jennifer — her hands stay ringed with her ordeals — but her fearless tigers will prance for ever.

## The contrast that organises the poem

Everything in the poem is built on **contrast**:

| Aunt Jennifer | Her tigers |
|---|---|
| fluttering, weak fingers | prancing, pacing with certainty |
| terrified | unafraid |
| mastered by ordeals | masters of their green world |
| weighed down by the wedding band | sleek, free |
| will die and lie still | will go on prancing |
| fears men (the uncle) | do not fear the men beneath the tree |

The tigers are the aunt's **alter ego** — the self she would be if she were free. Art is where she puts the spirit her life has crushed.

**In one breath:** every quality of the tigers is the opposite of the aunt's; they are the free self she can only embroider.

## Poetic devices

- **Symbolism:** the **tigers** (freedom, strength, fearlessness, the suppressed self); the **wedding band** (marriage as a burden, male domination); the **screen/panel** (art as escape and permanence); **ivory needle** (the aunt's delicate, confined domestic world).
- **Imagery:** visual (bright topaz, world of green), tactile (the heavy band, the needle hard to pull).
- **Alliteration:** repeated initial sounds in the phrases about the fluttering fingers and the prancing, proud tigers (identify in the text).
- **Metaphor:** the *massive weight* of the ring = the burden of marriage.
- **Transferred epithet:** *terrified hands* — the aunt is terrified, not her hands.
- **Contrast / irony:** the fearful maker of fearless tigers; the ring that outlasts her life.
- **Form:** three quatrains in **rhyming couplets** (aabb), regular rhythm — a neat, controlled form that mirrors the aunt's constrained life while the tigers leap inside it.
- **Tone:** sympathetic to the aunt, admiring of the tigers, quietly angry at the system.

**In one breath:** symbolism (tigers, ring), transferred epithet, alliteration, contrast and tidy rhyming couplets carry a feminist protest in miniature.

## Themes and the poet's message

- **Patriarchy and the oppression of women:** the wedding band, the uncle, the ordeals that master her.
- **Marriage as constraint:** not a partnership but a weight.
- **Fear and lost identity:** the aunt's terror persists even in death; her name is attached to the tigers, not to any life of her own.
- **Art as expression and escape:** the tigers carry the courage and freedom she cannot live; they are her protest and her legacy.
- **The permanence of art:** the artist dies, the work goes on.

Rich's message is a feminist one, made without a single slogan: a woman denied freedom pours her unlived self into her art, and the art — proud, unafraid — is both a monument to her spirit and an indictment of the world that crushed her.

**In one breath:** a woman mastered by marriage and fear creates tigers that are free; the poem honours her art and condemns her cage.
