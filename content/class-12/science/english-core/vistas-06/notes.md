# Memories of Childhood — Zitkala-Sa and Bama

Text: NCERT *Vistas*, Chapter 6 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)). Two autobiographical pieces by two women from two continents, written decades apart, placed together because they tell the **same story**: a child discovers that the world has decided she is **inferior** — for her race in one case, her caste in the other — and the child begins to **resist**. Both writers belong to **marginalised communities**, both write about **childhood**, and both found their way to **dignity through learning**.

## Part I — The Cutting of My Long Hair (Zitkala-Sa)

**Zitkala-Sa** (1876–1938), born Gertrude Simmons, was a **Native American** of the Dakota (Sioux) people. As a child she was taken to the **Carlisle Indian School** in Pennsylvania, one of the boarding schools set up to "civilise" Native children by stripping them of their language, dress and customs. This extract describes her **first day**.

### The first day: bells, shoes and eating by formula

The day is **bitterly cold**; snow covers the ground. A loud **bell** rings and the children are marched into the dining room. Everything is strange and harsh: the girls wear **stiff shoes** and **tight clinging dresses**, and the small Indian girl is uncomfortable and ashamed in them. Breakfast follows rules she does not know: a bell to pull out the chairs, a second bell to sit, a third to begin eating. She sits at the first bell and has to stand again while the others pray; a **paleface woman** stares at her. She calls this **eating by formula** — a phrase that sums up the school's mechanical discipline, where even a meal is drilled.

**In one breath:** on a freezing first morning Zitkala-Sa is marched by bells into a dining room where even eating follows rules — the first taste of regimentation.

### Judewin's warning

Her friend **Judewin**, who knows a little English, tells her she overheard the paleface woman: the girls' **long, heavy hair** is to be **cut**. In her culture this is a horror: among her people **short, shingled hair** is worn by **cowards**, and hair is **cut short on mourners**; long hair is dignity and identity. Judewin says they must **submit**, because the white people are strong. Zitkala-Sa says she will **struggle first**.

**In one breath:** Judewin warns that their hair will be cut; to Zitkala-Sa's people that is the mark of cowards and mourners, and she resolves to fight.

### Hiding, capture, the shears

She creeps upstairs into a large room with three white beds, crawls **under a bed** in the dark corner and hides, hearing footsteps and voices calling her name. The women and girls search the house; someone looks under the bed and **drags her out**, though she kicks and scratches. She is **carried downstairs**, **tied fast to a chair**, and she hears the **gnawing of the shears** on one of her **thick braids**. Then she **loses her spirit**. She remembers her mother's words and knows that no one will comfort her now; she feels like **one of many little animals driven by a herder**. Her resistance has been physically overpowered, and with her hair goes her sense of self.

:::tip Why the hair matters
The examiner wants the cultural meaning: in her community shingled hair marks cowards and is cut only in mourning. Cutting it was not a haircut but an attack on her **identity and dignity** — the point of the whole piece.
:::

**In one breath:** dragged from under a bed, tied to a chair and shorn, she feels her spirit leave her — one more animal in the herd.

### What the extract shows

The piece is a quiet indictment of **cultural oppression** dressed as education: discipline by bells, alien clothes, the forced cutting of hair, no comfort for a child. It also shows a child's **courage** — she hides, kicks and scratches — and its defeat. Zitkala-Sa grew up to be a writer and activist for Native American rights; this memory is where that fight began.

**In one breath:** the "civilising" school destroys a child's culture by force; her small rebellion is the seed of a life of resistance.

## Part II — We Too Are Human Beings (Bama)

**Bama** (born 1958) is a **Tamil Dalit** writer; this extract is from her autobiography **Karukku**. She describes an incident from her **third standard** — the day she first understood what **untouchability** meant.

### The walk home

Bama could walk home from school in ten minutes but took **thirty minutes to an hour**, because the street was full of things worth watching: the **performing monkey**, the **snake charmer**, the **cyclist** who had been riding for days without a break, the **Maariyaata temple** with its huge bell, the **Pongal** offerings being cooked, the dried-fish stall, the sweet stall and the man selling fried snacks, the political parties' street-corner speeches, the **puppet show** and the **stunt performances**. Her account is joyful — a child drinking in every sight — which makes the next scene sharper.

**In one breath:** little Bama dawdles home, feasting on monkeys, snake charmers, temple bells and sweet stalls — a child's happy street.

### The man with the packet

One day, in the **threshing floor** area, she sees a **landlord** seated on a piece of sacking, watching his workers threshing. Then an **elder** from her own street comes along from the bazaar, carrying a small **packet** by its **string**, not touching the packet itself, holding it out at arm's length. He goes up to the landlord, **bows low** and offers it; the landlord opens it and begins eating the **vadais**. Bama finds the sight hilarious — a grown man carrying a packet of snacks so strangely — and she laughs, running home to tell her brother.

**In one breath:** Bama laughs at an elder carrying a snack packet by its string and bowing to the landlord — not yet knowing what she has seen.

### Annan explains

Her elder brother, **Annan**, does not laugh. He explains that the man was not being funny: he and the landlord belong to different castes; the elder is from a **low caste**, and the **upper-caste** landlord would not eat food that a low-caste hand had touched — so the packet had to be carried by its string. The elder was obeying the rule of **untouchability**.

Bama's reaction turns from laughter to **anger** and grief. Why should people have to fetch and carry for others? What is so special about these landlords? The elder was an important man in her street, yet he had to bow and scrape for a few vadais. She feels **provoked**, wanting to touch the packet herself. It is the moment of **awakening**: a child understands that the humiliation she saw is meant for her too.

**In one breath:** Annan explains untouchability — the upper caste will not touch what a Dalit has touched — and Bama's laughter turns to anger.

### Annan's advice and Bama's answer

Annan himself has felt the humiliation: once, when he gave his name, a man asked which **street** he lived on — a way of asking his caste. He tells Bama that because they are born into this community, they are **never given honour or dignity**; but if they **study hard and make progress**, they can throw away these indignities, and **people will come to them of their own accord**. Bama takes the advice: she **studies hard**, with all her breath and being, and **stands first** in her class. Because of that, many people become her friends.

:::tip Education as resistance
Both parts end differently but point one way. Zitkala-Sa's resistance is physical and fails on the day; Bama's is through study and succeeds. For a 5-mark comparison, note that both women eventually fought their oppression with writing and education.
:::

**In one breath:** Annan says study is the way out of indignity; Bama studies, tops her class and wins friends — resistance through achievement.

## Themes shared by the two parts

- **Discrimination against the marginalised:** racial oppression of Native Americans; caste oppression of Dalits.
- **The child's eye:** both writers see injustice first with a child's confusion — Zitkala-Sa's terror, Bama's laughter — and then understand it.
- **Identity and dignity:** long hair as identity; untouchability as the denial of human equality (the title of Bama's piece says it: we too are human beings).
- **Resistance:** hiding and struggling; studying and excelling; later, writing.
- **The role of a guide:** Judewin counsels submission; Annan counsels education — and Bama's choice shows which works.
- **Autobiography as protest:** both women turned childhood memory into a public case against injustice.
