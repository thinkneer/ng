# The Interview — Christopher Silvester and Mukund Padmanabhan

Text: NCERT *Flamingo*, Chapter 7 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)). The chapter has two parts. **Part I** is an extract from Christopher Silvester's introduction to *The Penguin Book of Interviews*: a short history of the interview and a survey of what famous people have thought of it, mostly unflattering. **Part II** is an actual interview — **Mukund Padmanabhan** of *The Hindu* talking to the Italian scholar-novelist **Umberto Eco**. Together they let you see the form being discussed and then the form being practised, by a skilled interviewer and a generous subject.

## Part I — A short history of the interview

The interview as a journalistic form is about **130 years old** (counting from when Silvester wrote; it was invented in the 1860s). Since then it has become a **commonplace of journalism**; thousands of celebrities have been interviewed, some many times over. **Opinions of it differ widely.** Some see it as a **source of truth** and, in its practice, an **art**. Others, especially celebrities who have been its victims, think of it as an unwarranted **intrusion** into their lives, something that **diminishes** them — like the primitive belief that having one's photograph taken steals a bit of one's soul.

:::tip Two camps in one sentence
2-mark questions ask for the "differing opinions": (1) a source of truth and an art; (2) an intrusion that diminishes the subject. Give both sides with one example each.
:::

**In one breath:** the interview is 130 years old and everywhere; some call it truth and art, others an intrusion that diminishes them.

## The celebrities who hated it

Silvester quotes a line of famous writers who despised being interviewed, and the examiner likes to ask about each:

- **V. S. Naipaul** felt that some people are **wounded** by interviews and **lose a part of themselves**.
- **Lewis Carroll**, author of *Alice in Wonderland*, had a **just horror of the interviewer** and never consented to one; his refusals made him famous as a man who would not be interviewed, which only amused the interviewers more.
- **Rudyard Kipling** was even more condemning. His wife **Caroline** recorded in her diary (**Boston, 1892**) that two reporters had ruined their day. Kipling called interviewing **immoral**, a **crime**, an **assault** that deserved punishment, something no respectable man would ask or give — yet, the author notes, Kipling himself had once **interviewed Mark Twain**. The hypocrisy is part of the point.
- **H. G. Wells** in 1894 called it an **ordeal** he dreaded, though he was frequently interviewed — and forty years later, in **1934**, he was himself **interviewing Joseph Stalin**.
- **Saul Bellow**, who consented to interviews on several occasions, described them as **thumbprints on his windpipe** — the feeling of being choked.

## Why the interview matters all the same

Despite the drawbacks, Silvester insists on the interview's value: it is a **supremely serviceable medium of communication**. He quotes **Denis Brian**, who points out that almost everything of moment today reaches us **through one person asking questions of another**; because of this the **interviewer holds a position of unprecedented power and influence**. The interview is how we actually learn about our contemporaries — their minds, their opinions, their lives.

## Part II — Umberto Eco in conversation

**Umberto Eco** (1932–2016) was a professor at the **University of Bologna**, famous among scholars for his work on **semiotics** (the study of signs), literary interpretation and medieval aesthetics, and known to the whole world for his novel **The Name of the Rose** (1980), a medieval murder mystery that sold between **ten and fifteen million copies**. Mukund Padmanabhan's interview explores one puzzle: how does one man produce more than **forty scholarly works**, five novels, children's books and newspaper columns — and why did a difficult, learned novel become a bestseller?

**In one breath:** Eco, a semiotics professor at Bologna, wrote forty-plus academic books and a surprise bestselling novel; the interview asks how and why.

## The secret of "interstices"

Asked how he manages to do so much, Eco says he has a secret, like everyone — he works in **"interstices"**, the **empty spaces** of life. His example: suppose a visitor is coming up to his flat in the lift; while the lift rises, Eco is waiting — that waiting time is an interstice, and he uses it to write. He widens the idea with a scientific image: if all the empty space inside the atoms of the universe were removed, the universe would shrink to the size of a fist — our lives, too, are mostly empty spaces, and what others waste, he fills. He also tells Padmanabhan he does not feel he is doing so many different things: he keeps the **same philosophical and ethical interests** in his scholarly writing, his children's books and his novels, so it is **one work in many forms**.

:::

## Scholarship told as a story

Padmanabhan notes that Eco's **academic writing** has a **playful, personal, narrative** quality unusual in scholars, whose style is usually dry and depersonalised. Eco agrees. He explains that at **22**, when he presented his **doctoral dissertation**, one professor told him that scholars usually hide the trials and errors of research and present only the conclusions, whereas Eco had **told the story of his research**, with its false starts. The professor meant it as praise — and published the dissertation. From that moment Eco understood that **scholarly books can be written by telling a story**, and he says that is why his essays read like narratives and why, perhaps, at **fifty** he turned to fiction. Novels came to him almost by **accident**: he had nothing to do one day, so he started writing one.

## "I am a university professor who writes novels on Sundays"

Padmanabhan asks whether Eco minds being known mainly as a novelist. Eco says he identifies himself first as an **academic**; he takes part in academic conferences, not in meetings of **Pen clubs** and writers. He has written forty scholarly works against five novels and considers the novels a small part of his output, though he knows that if he is remembered, it will be as a novelist. His self-description — a professor who writes novels at the weekend — is the examiner's favourite quotation of the chapter.

**In one breath:** Eco calls himself an academic first and a novelist second — forty scholarly books against five novels.

## Why did The Name of the Rose succeed?

The interviewer raises the mystery: *The Name of the Rose* is a **serious novel**, a **detective story** that delves into **metaphysics, theology and medieval history**, hardly the stuff of bestsellers — and yet it sold ten to fifteen million copies. Eco's answer is modest. He says the book is a **mystery** to him as well. His **publisher** expected to sell no more than **three thousand** copies in Italy, where few read and fewer read in the Middle Ages. Perhaps **timing** had something to do with it — had he written it ten years earlier or later, it might have failed; the public was ready then. Journalists and publishers, he adds, assume that people like trash, but many readers want **difficult reading experiences**, and the novel found them. Eco refuses to take credit for a formula: the success remains a mystery.

## Themes and what to take from the chapter

- **The interview as a form:** its history, its power (Brian), its critics (Naipaul to Bellow), and its paradox — those who hate being interviewed interview others.
- **Privacy versus the public's right to know:** the celebrity's sense of intrusion against the reader's hunger for truth about public figures.
- **A good interview in action:** Padmanabhan asks short, well-prepared questions, follows up, and lets Eco talk; Eco answers with stories and images. Students can use Part II as a model for their own interviews in the ASL/project work.
- **Eco's lessons on work and writing:** use the interstices; keep one set of interests across many forms; scholarship can tell a story; success cannot be manufactured.
