# My Mother at Sixty-six — Kamala Das

Text: NCERT *Flamingo*, Poem 1 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)). Read the poem twice from the textbook before this guide — it is short (a single long sentence), and the questions depend on knowing which image comes where. Kamala Das (1934–2009), one of India's best-known English-language poets, writes here about something every one of us will face: the moment you realise a parent is growing old.

## What happens in the poem

The poem is one continuous thought. The poet is **driving from her parent's home to Cochin airport** on a Friday morning, her mother **dozing beside her**. She looks at the sleeping face — mouth open, the colour drained from it — and it strikes her that her mother looks like a **corpse**. The thought hurts, so she **looks away**, out of the window, at the **young trees** that seem to be sprinting past the car and at the **children** pouring out of their homes to play. At the airport, after the **security check**, she looks back at her mother standing a few yards away — **pale** now, like the **late winter's moon** — and her **old familiar ache**, the **childhood fear** of losing her mother, returns. But she says nothing of it. She **smiles and smiles**, telling her mother she will see her soon.

That is the whole poem: a look, a fear, a turning away, a second look, and a smile that hides everything.

## The two faces of the mother

The poem gives two pictures of the mother, and the examiner loves to ask about both:

1. **In the car:** dozing with her mouth open, her face **ashen** — the grey of ash, lifeless — which the poet compares to a **corpse**. The simile is shocking on purpose; it is the daughter's sudden, unwanted recognition that her mother is nearing death.
2. **At the airport:** standing a few yards away, **wan and pale**, compared to the **late winter's moon**. The winter moon is dim, colourless, often hidden by mist, and winter is the season of the year's end — all of which suggest age, fading and the approach of death.

Both comparisons say the same thing in two registers: the first is raw (a dead body), the second is softer and more poetic (a fading moon), as if the poet has steadied herself a little between the two looks.

:::tip "Ashen" and "wan"
Students lose marks by writing that the mother "was sick". She is not ill; she is old. Use the poem's own key words — *ashen*, *corpse-like*, *wan, pale*, *late winter's moon* — and explain them as signs of age and approaching death.
:::

## The world outside the window: contrast

Why does the poet look out of the window? To **escape the painful thought**. And what does she see? **Young trees sprinting** and **merry children spilling out of their homes** — images of **youth, energy, movement and life**. The trees do not really sprint; the car's motion makes them seem to race backwards, but the word also gives them the energy of young runners. The children are the beginning of life, the opposite of the mother's end.

This is the poem's central **contrast**: inside the car, age, stillness, a face like death; outside, youth, speed, laughter. The poet puts them side by side so that the mother's age is felt more sharply — and so that we see the daughter trying to distract herself with life.

## The childhood fear

At the airport the poet feels her **old familiar ache** — a **childhood fear**. Every child fears losing its mother; the fear usually fades as we grow up and the parent seems permanent. Here it returns, because the poet now sees that the separation it dreaded is genuinely coming: not today's goodbye at the airport but the final one. The word *familiar* matters — this is not a new feeling but one she has carried all her life, suddenly awake again.

**In one breath:** the parting revives the poet's lifelong childhood fear of losing her mother, now made real by her mother's visible age.

## The smile

The poem ends with the poet smiling **again and again** as she says goodbye and promises to see her mother soon. The repetition is the key. One smile would be natural; the repeated smile is **effort** — a mask the daughter puts on to **hide her fear**, to **reassure her mother**, and perhaps to reassure herself. There is also the hope in the promise: she wants to believe there will be another meeting. Grief, love, pretence and hope are packed into that single repeated word, and the poem's single sentence ends on it.

:::tip Why "smile, smile, smile"?
Three reasons the examiner expects: (1) to hide her own pain and fear; (2) to put on a brave face and comfort her mother, who should not see her anxiety at parting; (3) the promise to meet again expresses hope. Repetition shows the smile is forced, not spontaneous.
:::

## Poetic devices you must be able to name

- **Simile:** the mother's face compared to a *corpse*; her paleness to the *late winter's moon*.
- **Imagery:** visual images of the ashen face, the sprinting trees, the children spilling out, the pale moon.
- **Contrast / juxtaposition:** age and death inside the car versus youth and life outside.
- **Personification:** trees that *sprint* — given the action of young runners.
- **Repetition:** the smile repeated three times, showing effort and emotion held back.
- **Alliteration:** can be found in the sound patterns of the lines on the children and the moon (identify from the text).
- **Symbolism:** the winter moon (age, fading life); children and young trees (youth, continuity of life); the airport (departure, separation, mortality).
- **Form:** a single long sentence in **free verse** with no stanza break — the unbroken flow of a thought the poet cannot stop; enjambment throughout.
- **Tone:** quiet, reflective, tender, with an undercurrent of fear.

## Themes and the poet's message

- **Ageing and mortality:** parents grow old; a child must face that they will die.
- **Fear of separation and loss:** the childhood ache that never fully goes.
- **Love and helplessness:** the daughter can do nothing about time; all she can do is smile.
- **Putting on a brave face:** emotions hidden for the sake of someone we love.
- **The cycle of life:** the children outside will one day sit where the poet sits.

The poem's message is not despair. It accepts the inevitability of ageing and death while showing the dignity of love that hides its fear to spare the beloved.

**In one breath:** a daughter's love confronts a mother's ageing; the poem accepts mortality and honours the brave smile that love wears.
