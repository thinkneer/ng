# The Tiger King — Kalki

Text: NCERT *Vistas*, Chapter 2 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)). Kalki (R. Krishnamurthy, 1899–1954) was a Tamil writer, journalist and freedom fighter, and this is a **satire**: a comic story with a serious target. The target is the **arrogance of power** — kings who think they can defeat fate, flatterers who tell them what they want to hear, and the British who could be bought with jewellery. The humour comes from **exaggeration** and **irony**; the sting comes at the end, when the mightiest tiger hunter in India is killed by a toy.

## The prophecy at birth

The Maharaja of **Pratibandapuram** — his grand full title runs to many words, so he is simply the **Tiger King** — was, at birth, the subject of a prophecy. The **chief astrologer** predicted that the child would grow up to be a great warrior and hero, but would **die one day**. Everyone who is born must die, the narrator comments — a joke at the astrologer's expense. Then the **ten-day-old baby spoke**: he asked how he would die and challenged the astrologer. The astrologer, astonished, explained that the child was born in the hour of the **Bull**; the bull and the tiger are enemies, so **death would come from a tiger**. The baby growled that tigers should beware.

Kalki's tone is set: a talking infant, a solemn astrologer, and a destiny that the story will fulfil in the most absurd way possible.

**In one breath:** at birth an astrologer predicts death by a tiger; the ten-day-old prince answers that tigers should beware — the comic, fatal challenge begins.

## The hunt begins

The prince grew up like other royal children of the time — **English nurse, English tutor, English films** — and became king at twenty. He remembered the prophecy and asked the astrologer again. The astrologer added a detail: the king could kill **ninety-nine tigers**, but must be very careful with the **hundredth**. If the king killed the hundredth and survived, the astrologer promised to **cut off his tuft, crop his hair short and become an insurance agent** — a joke that fixes the number one hundred in everyone's mind.

The Maharaja began a **tiger-killing campaign**. He killed his first tiger and was delighted. He made it a **royal monopoly**: only he could hunt tigers in Pratibandapuram; anyone else who threw even a stone at a tiger would lose all his wealth. The state's forests were fairly full, and over ten years he killed **seventy**. His single-mindedness was total: when a tiger escaped him he pursued it; when the dewan warned him that hunting could be dangerous he brushed it aside. The narrator notes the Maharaja's good luck — the only time he came close to death was when a tiger sprang on him, but he killed it with his gun (or his sword, in another near-miss).

## The British officer and the fifty rings

Trouble came in the shape of a **high-ranking British officer** who wanted to hunt tigers in Pratibandapuram. The Maharaja refused him permission — and even refused the compromise of letting the officer be **photographed holding a gun** beside a tiger the Maharaja had killed. Refusing a British officer could cost a king his throne, so the Maharaja and his dewan found a remedy: they sent **fifty samples of expensive diamond rings** to the officer's **wife**, expecting her to choose one or two. The lady kept **all fifty** and sent a letter of thanks. The bill came to **three lakh rupees** — but the Maharaja kept his kingdom and his tiger monopoly.

:::tip Why the rings matter
Two points for the examiner: (1) satire on the greed of the British officials and the way Indian rulers bought their safety; (2) the Maharaja's extravagance — three lakhs spent to protect a hobby. Mention the number fifty and the wife keeping them all.
:::

**In one breath:** to pacify a British officer denied a hunt, the king sends fifty diamond rings to the officer's wife, who keeps them all — three lakhs for a tiger monopoly.

## Marriage for tigers

After ten years the tigers of Pratibandapuram were **extinct**, and the king had only seventy. Where were the other thirty to come from? The dewan was ordered to find a **bride** — but not any bride: the Maharaja asked for a princess from a state with **a large tiger population**. The dewan found one, and the Maharaja married her. On each visit to his father-in-law's kingdom he killed five or six tigers, and so the count reached **ninety-nine**. Marriage, in this story, is a hunting licence.

**In one breath:** when his own forests run out of tigers, he marries into a tiger-rich state and hunts the count up to ninety-nine.

## The hundredth tiger

Now the Maharaja's anxiety became unbearable; he thought of nothing but the hundredth tiger. But his father-in-law's tigers had also become extinct. Then a report came: **sheep** were disappearing from a hillside village, which could mean a tiger. The king announced a **three-year tax exemption** for the village and set off. No tiger appeared. In fury he **doubled the land tax** and dismissed many officers; the dewan feared for his own job.

The dewan found a solution: an **old tiger** from the **People's Park in Madras**, which he and his wife dragged out and pushed into the forest where the king was hunting. The tiger wandered towards the king, who shot it; it collapsed. The Maharaja was overjoyed — the hundredth tiger, the prophecy defeated — and returned to his capital in triumph. But the **hunters** found the tiger **alive**: the bullet had **missed**, and the old beast had merely **fainted** in shock. Afraid of losing their jobs, they shot it themselves and carried it in procession through the town. The king, unknowing, held a grand burial for it and even had a tomb built.

:::tip The hundredth tiger was not killed by the king
The irony is layered: the hundredth kill was a sick zoo tiger, the king missed it, and his servants killed it secretly. So the king never really killed his hundredth tiger — and the prophecy still had to be fulfilled.
:::

**In one breath:** the "hundredth tiger" is an old zoo animal that the king misses and his hunters quietly kill; the king celebrates a victory he did not win.

## The wooden tiger

A few days later it was the king's son's **third birthday**. The Maharaja, who had never had time for his son, wanted a special gift, and in a toyshop he found a **wooden tiger** — the shopkeeper asked only two annas and a quarter but, since royalty cannot buy anything cheap, the price was raised to three hundred rupees. The toy was badly made by an **unskilled carpenter**: its rough surface was covered with tiny **slivers** of wood. As father and son played with it, a sliver **pierced the Maharaja's right hand**. He pulled it out, but the next day the wound turned into a suppurating **sore** and spread up the arm. **Three surgeons** were brought from Madras; they operated, and announced that the **operation was successful** — the Maharaja was **dead**. The hundredth tiger had taken its revenge.

## What the story satirises

- **Arrogance of rulers:** a king who challenges fate, keeps a monopoly on tigers, doubles taxes in a fit of anger, raises a toy's price to suit his dignity, and never has time for his child.
- **Flattery and fear at court:** the dewan and the hunters lie to keep their jobs; the astrologer hedges his prophecy with a joke.
- **British–Indian relations:** the king fears a British officer more than a tiger, and buys him off through his wife's greed.
- **Senseless killing of wildlife:** a hundred tigers slaughtered to prove a point; two states' tiger populations wiped out. The story reads today as an early plea for conservation.
- **Fate vs free will:** the harder the king fights the prophecy, the more precisely it comes true — through a wooden tiger.
- **Hypocrisy of ceremony:** the "operation successful, patient dead" line mocks pomp that hides failure.

**Devices:** **dramatic irony** (we know what the king does not), **hyperbole** (the talking baby, fifty rings, three hundred rupees for a toy), **humour**, **satire**, and a **twist ending** that fulfils the prophecy literally.

Characters: **the Tiger King** (arrogant, obsessive, wilful), **the astrologer**, **the dewan** (frightened, resourceful flatterer), **the British officer and his wife**, **the hunters**, **the surgeons**.
