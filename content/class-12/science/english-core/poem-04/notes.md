# A Roadside Stand — Robert Frost

Text: NCERT *Flamingo*, Poem 4 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)). Robert Frost (1874–1963), America's great poet of rural New England, here turns angry. The poem is about a small farm stall on a highway and the city traffic that speeds past it, and underneath that about the **gap between rural poverty and urban wealth**, the **false promises** made to the poor, and the poet's own **pity** — which, in the last lines, he turns on himself.

## The shed by the highway

A farm family has built a **little new shed** at the edge of their house, right beside the road, where the **traffic** flows past. They do not ask for charity — Frost is clear that it is not bread they beg — but for a little **city money**, the cash that flows through the cities and keeps them alive, so that the farmers too might feel a share of the **flower of cities** that the newspapers and films promise is the good life. They offer **wild berries in wooden quarts** and **crook-necked golden squash with silver warts**, and even the beauty of the mountain scene — honest country goods, priced for passers-by.

**In one breath:** poor farmers build a roadside stall hoping the city traffic will stop and spend a little of its money on their berries and squash.

## The polished traffic passes

The **polished traffic** — the shiny cars of the well-off — **passes with a mind ahead**, intent on its destination. If a car does stop, it is almost never to buy. The drivers stop to **complain**: the stand's crude signs with their letters **N and S turned wrong** spoil the landscape; the painted shed is an eyesore against the beautiful mountain background. Or they stop to **ask the way**, or to **turn the car around** in the yard, or to ask for a **gallon of gas** that the farmers do not sell. Each stop raises hope and leaves nothing.

:::tip Why the cars stop
A favourite 2-mark question. Four reasons, none of them buying: to complain about the signs/the shed spoiling the view; to ask the way; to use the yard to turn round; to ask for a gallon of gas.
:::

Frost calls the hope of the farmers a **childish longing** — not to mock it but to show how innocent and unrealistic it is: they wait all day for the sound of a car slowing, the squeal of brakes, the sight of a hand stretched out with money.

## The greedy good-doers

The poem's bitterest stanza is about those who claim to help. The news is that these **pitiful kin** (the poor rural folk) are to be **bought out** and **relocated** in villages **next to the theatre and the store**, where they need not think for themselves. Frost calls these helpers **greedy good-doers** and **beneficent beasts of prey** — oxymorons that say the do-gooders are predators in disguise. They **swarm** over the lives of the poor, **enforcing benefits** that are **calculated** to **soothe them out of their wits**; they teach them to **sleep all day** and destroy their natural **sleep at night**, the ancient way of life.

Frost's target is a kind of government or corporate "development" that uproots people from the land and their independence, makes them dependent consumers, and profits from them while pretending to be charity.

:::tip Explain the oxymorons
"Greedy good-doers" and "beneficent beasts of prey" are **oxymorons**: two contradictory words joined. Say what each half means — *good-doers / beneficent* (appearing kind) versus *greedy / beasts of prey* (actually exploiting) — and that Frost uses them to expose false philanthropy.
:::

## The poet's pain

Frost admits that he can hardly bear the thought of the farmers' **childish longing in vain**: the open window waiting all day for a car to slow, the sadness that **lurks** near the open window, the hope that dies every time a car passes. He describes the rare car that does stop — only to ask for gas — and the farmers' angry retort that they do not sell it.

Then comes the double-edged ending. The poet says he cannot bear their pain; at times he thinks the kindest thing would be to **put them out of their pain at one stroke** — a terrible thought. But then he **turns it on himself**: if he, in his own city comfort, were ever in such pain, would he not also want someone to end it? The reversal is a confession — pity for the poor is easy; sharing their place is not. The poem ends on this uneasy honesty rather than a solution.

:::tip The ending is not a death wish
Students misread the last lines as Frost wanting the farmers dead. The point is ironic self-criticism: the poet catches himself thinking a cruel "merciful" thought and recognises that he would resent such mercy if it were aimed at him. Say "irony" and "self-reflection".
:::

## Poetic devices

- **Imagery:** the shed, the wooden quarts of berries, the golden squash with silver warts, the open window, the squeal of brakes.
- **Alliteration:** repeated initial sounds run through the poem (the lines on the polished traffic, the greedy good-doers, the beneficent beasts — spot them in the text).
- **Personification:** the traffic passes *with a mind ahead*; sadness *lurks* near the window.
- **Transferred epithet:** the cars are called **selfish**, though it is the people in them who are selfish.
- **Oxymoron:** *greedy good-doers*, *beneficent beasts of prey*.
- **Metaphor:** the *flower of cities* (urban prosperity); *city money* (the cash economy).
- **Irony:** help that hurts; the poet's "mercy" turned on himself.
- **Tone:** sympathetic to the farmers, satirical towards the traffic and the do-gooders, self-questioning at the end.
- **Form:** a long single stanza with irregular rhyme, conversational blank-verse rhythm.

## Themes and the poet's message

- **Rural–urban divide:** the poor who grow food and the rich who speed past them.
- **Economic inequality and the lure of money:** the farmers' longing for a share of the cash that passes them.
- **False philanthropy:** development that destroys independence; the greedy good-doers.
- **Indifference:** the polished traffic with its mind ahead.
- **Empathy and its limits:** the poet's pain, and his honesty about the ease of pity.

Frost's message: the rural poor deserve a fair share of the nation's wealth and real respect, not the indifference of passing traffic or the calculated charity of those who would uproot them.

**In one breath:** a roadside stand becomes Frost's protest against indifference and false charity towards the rural poor — and a check on his own easy pity.
