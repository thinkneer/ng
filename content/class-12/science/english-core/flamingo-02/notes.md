# Lost Spring — Anees Jung

Text: NCERT *Flamingo*, Chapter 2 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)). The chapter has two parts, each built around one child: **Saheb**, a ragpicker in Delhi, and **Mukesh**, a bangle maker's son in Firozabad. The title says what both lose: *spring*, the season of childhood, growth and hope. The subtitle calls these "stories of stolen childhood".

## Part 1 — Saheb, the ragpicker of Seemapuri

The author meets **Saheb-e-Alam** every morning scrounging for "gold" in the garbage dumps of her neighbourhood. His name means **"lord of the universe"**, an irony he does not understand: he is barefoot, poor and homeless. His family came from **Dhaka** (Bangladesh), where storms had swept away their fields and homes, so they migrated to Delhi in search of a living.

The author's first conversation shows how casually the comfortable make promises to the poor. She asks why he does not go to school; he says there is no school in his neighbourhood. She half-jokingly says she will start one, and he returns days later asking whether it is ready. She is embarrassed: she, too, has made a promise she never meant to keep, like so many others.

**In one breath:** Saheb, a Bangladeshi migrant ragpicker whose grand name mocks his poverty, exposes how easily the well-off make empty promises.

## Barefoot children and the excuse of tradition

The author notices an army of barefoot ragpicker boys. One says it is **tradition** to go barefoot; she suspects this is an excuse that hides **perpetual poverty**. She recalls a man from Udipi who, as a boy, prayed at the temple for a pair of shoes — thirty years later the priest's son wears shoes, and many children in that town have them, yet in the ragpickers' colony the children remain shoeless. The point: lack of shoes is not culture; it is the mark of a poverty that does not go away for everyone.

**In one breath:** going barefoot is called tradition, but it is really the uniform of lasting poverty.

## Seemapuri: garbage as gold

Saheb's home is **Seemapuri**, a settlement on the edge of Delhi, geographically close yet "miles away" in condition: mud structures with roofs of tin and tarpaulin, no sewage, no drainage, no running water. About 10,000 ragpickers live there, migrants from Bangladesh in 1971. They have **ration cards** and names on voters' lists — that matters more to them than an identity, because it gets them food. Wherever there is food, they pitch their tents; survival, not belonging, decides where they live.

For the adults, **garbage is gold**: it is their daily bread and the roof over their heads. For the children it is something more — it is wrapped in **wonder**; sometimes a boy finds a rupee or even a ten-rupee note and hopes to find more. This difference between the adult's necessity and the child's hope is one of the chapter's most-asked points.

**In one breath:** Seemapuri is a slum of 10,000 Bangladeshi migrants where garbage is the adults' livelihood and the children's treasure hunt.

## Saheb loses his freedom

Saheb watches tennis at the club gate; a boy in a white shirt plays while he stands outside. He has got discarded **tennis shoes** with a hole — to a boy who has walked barefoot, even these are a dream. Later the author sees him carrying a **steel canister** to a **tea stall**, where he now earns 800 rupees and all his meals. But his face has lost its carefree look. The canister is heavier than the plastic bag he used to carry so lightly: the bag was his, the canister belongs to the tea-stall owner. **Saheb is no longer his own master.** Regular wages have cost him his freedom.

The examiner looks for the contrast: the plastic bag was his own (freedom, however poor); the steel canister belongs to his employer (security without freedom). Name both objects in your answer.
:::

**In one breath:** at the tea stall Saheb earns money and meals but loses the freedom of the ragpicker — the canister is heavier than the bag.

## Part 2 — Mukesh, the bangle maker who wants a garage

The second story moves to **Firozabad**, the centre of India's **glass bangle** industry, where every other family works in the trade. **Mukesh** insists on being his own master; he wants to be a **motor mechanic**, and he will walk to a garage and learn. In a town where sons simply follow fathers, this dream is an act of rebellion.

The author describes the work: families sit around flickering **furnaces** with high temperatures, in dingy cells without air or light, welding glass and making bangles. Children work beside their parents, often losing the brightness of their eyes before they are adults; many become blind, since the law banning child labour is ignored. About 20,000 children work in the glass furnaces of Firozabad.

**In one breath:** Firozabad's bangle makers labour in dark, hot cells; children lose their eyesight; Mukesh alone dares to dream of a garage.

## Mukesh's home and family

Mukesh's lane stinks of garbage; homes are half-built hovels with wobbly doors and crumbling walls. His house has a thatched roof, a firewood stove and a frail young woman — his elder brother's wife — cooking; she is already the **bahu**, in charge of the household though hardly older than Mukesh. His father, an old man with failing eyesight, first a tailor then a bangle maker, has worked all his life and has not managed to renovate the house or send his sons to school; all he has taught them is the art of bangle making.

Mukesh's **grandmother** believes it is their **karam**, their **destiny**: born to a caste of bangle makers, they cannot imagine any other work. This belief — a "god-given lineage" — is the mental chain the chapter exposes.

**In one breath:** Mukesh's family has worked bangles for generations and gained nothing; his grandmother calls it destiny.

## Savita and the old woman

The author sees **Savita**, a young girl in drab pink, soldering pieces of glass with mechanical speed. She does not yet know the **sanctity of bangles**: they symbolise an Indian woman's **suhaag**, her married state. One day she will wear them as a bride, like the old woman beside her who still has bangles on her wrist but no light in her eyes — the old woman has not had one full meal in her life, and her husband, a bangle maker who knows nothing else, has given her only a roof. The irony: the makers of a symbol of joy never know joy themselves.

**In one breath:** Savita makes bangles whose meaning she does not know; the old woman beside her shows where that life ends — bangles on the wrist, hunger in the stomach.

## The vicious circle

Why do they not organise themselves into a **cooperative**? Because, the young men say, they would be hauled up by the police, beaten and jailed for doing something illegal. There is no leader among them; they are trapped in a **vicious circle** of **sahukars** (moneylenders), **middlemen**, **policemen**, **keepers of law**, **bureaucrats** and **politicians**. The author sees two distinct worlds in Firozabad: the family caught in poverty and the stigma of caste, and the circle of exploiters around it. Together they impose a **baggage** on the child he cannot put down: he accepts it as naturally as his father did.

Yet in Mukesh the author finds a **flash of daring**. He wants to be a mechanic. When she asks if he dreams of flying a plane, he is embarrassed — that is too far; **few airplanes fly over Firozabad**. His dream is small enough to be real, which is why it matters.

:::

**In one breath:** the bangle makers are trapped by moneylenders, middlemen, police and politicians, and by their own belief in destiny; Mukesh's small dream of a garage is the one crack of light.

## Themes and the writer's message

- **Stolen childhood.** Both boys work when they should play and learn; the title's lost spring is the lost childhood of poor children.
- **Poverty and migration.** Saheb's family lost their fields in Bangladesh; Seemapuri exists because people go where food is.
- **Child labour and law.** Laws exist (child labour is banned; a cooperative is legal) but are not enforced; the poor fear the law more than they are protected by it.
- **Tradition and destiny as chains.** "Going barefoot is tradition", "bangle making is our karam" — beliefs that keep the poor from imagining change.
- **Freedom versus security.** Saheb's canister: a job can take away a child's freedom without giving him a childhood.
- **Hope.** Mukesh's dream and Saheb's wonder in the garbage are the human spirit refusing to die.

The author's **style**: first-person reportage, real names and places, telling details (the canister, the bangles on the old woman's wrist), irony (Saheb's name, bangle makers without joy), and questions that implicate the reader.

**In one breath:** Anees Jung shows two children robbed of childhood by poverty, tradition and exploitation — and the small dreams that survive.
