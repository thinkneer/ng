# Going Places — A. R. Barton

Text: NCERT *Flamingo*, Chapter 8 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)). The last prose chapter is the one students find closest to their own lives: a teenage girl from a working-class family who dreams far beyond her means, and the small, sad collision between her **fantasies** and her **reality**. The title is a pun — "going places" means being destined for success, and Sophie believes she is; the story shows how far she actually gets.

## Sophie and Jansie: two ways of leaving school

**Sophie** and **Jansie** are classmates about to leave school. Walking home, Sophie announces that she will have a **boutique** when she grows up — the most amazing shop the city has seen. Jansie, who sees things as they are, points out that a boutique takes money. Sophie says she will be a **manager** first, or an **actress** with a boutique on the side, or a **fashion designer**, something a bit sophisticated. Jansie reminds her that both of them are earmarked for the **biscuit factory**, and that Sophie should be sensible.

The contrast is the story's frame. Sophie is a **dreamer** who lives in possibilities; Jansie is **realistic**, accepting the limits of their class. The author does not mock Sophie — her longing is natural — but he lets us see how little ground her dreams stand on.

:::tip Sophie vs Jansie in one line
Sophie: imaginative, ambitious, escapist, refuses the biscuit factory. Jansie: practical, down-to-earth, knows the limits, warns Sophie. Most 2-mark questions on the two girls want exactly this contrast.
:::

## Sophie's home

Sophie's home is small and crowded. Her **father** is a plump, hearty man who comes home sweaty from work, eats shepherd's pie at the table, and is a passionate **football** fan — he is also quick to disbelieve his daughter and quicker to find fault with her. Her **mother** is worn by housework, her back stooped over the sink; the steamy little kitchen with its clutter of dishes is the opposite of Sophie's imagined boutique. Her little brother **Derek** is a child who says what he sees. And there is **Geoff**.

**In one breath:** a cramped, working-class home — a football-mad father, a tired mother, a frank little brother and a silent elder brother.

## Geoff: the brother she wants to follow

**Geoff**, three years older, left school to be an **apprentice mechanic**; he travels to work each day to the far side of the city. He is **tall, strong and silent**, speaking little. Sophie is fascinated by his silence: she suspects he has **areas of his life** she knows nothing about, places and people she has never seen, and she is **jealous** of that unknown world. She longs to be admitted to it — to ride **pillion** on his motorcycle in new shining black leather, into a world where she would be **welcomed and loved**. Geoff is the only one she tells her secrets to, because he is the only one who does not laugh at once.

**In one breath:** Sophie idolises her quiet brother Geoff and longs to enter the grown-up world his silence hides.

## The fantasy: meeting Danny Casey

The family's shared passion is football, and the family's hero is **Danny Casey**, a young **Irish** prodigy who plays for **United**. Every Saturday Sophie, Geoff and their father go to watch him. Sophie's daydream attaches itself to him. She tells Geoff that she **met Danny Casey** that day, in the **arcade**, outside **Royce's** window, and that he was kind — he spoke to her, told her about the shop, said he was going to buy a new one. Pressed for details by Geoff, she adds that he has **green eyes**, that he is **not very tall**, and that he asked her for an **autograph** — no, that she asked for one but they had no paper; and finally that he agreed to **meet her again** next week.

Geoff is sceptical but half-wants to believe. When he tells their **father**, the father dismisses it with a snort: one of these days she is going to get herself into **trouble** with her **wild stories**. He is more interested in his own tale — he once saw Danny Casey play against the Tottenham side and the man was unstoppable.

Here is the story's finest psychological touch: once Sophie has told the story, she **half believes it herself**; the lie becomes a plan, and she begins to look forward to the date.

## Jansie finds out

Geoff tells his friend **Frank**, and Frank tells **Jansie**, who taxes Sophie with it on the way to school. Sophie is dismayed: she had wanted the story kept to the family, and now Jansie, who is **"nosey"**, will spread it round the neighbourhood. Jansie is in fact sensible, almost kind: she says the whole thing is nonsense, that Casey would never look at Sophie, and Sophie wavers — she tells Jansie there was nothing in it, it was Geoff's exaggeration, just a word or two in the arcade. Sophie's fear of Jansie gossiping shows that, somewhere, she knows the story cannot bear daylight.

**In one breath:** the story leaks through Frank to Jansie; Sophie's panic at the leak betrays her knowledge that it is not true.

## Waiting by the canal

The following week Sophie goes to the place she has chosen for the meeting — a **wooden bench** beneath a solitary elm by the **canal**, where the two of them used to come as children. She sits and waits. She imagines Danny coming through the arcade, imagines Jansie's disbelief fading, imagines the whole town seeing her with him. Time passes. The light fades. She is sad, and she knows the sadness of the future: he will not come, and she will have to tell Geoff that he did not come, and watch his face, and see the hope in him die. Yet she still **sees him coming**, hears his footsteps, pictures his green eyes — the fantasy continues even as reality fails to show up.

:::tip Don't say "she lied and got caught"
The canal scene is not about punishment. The examiner wants: her loneliness, the gap between dream and reality, her awareness of what it will do to Geoff, and the persistence of the fantasy even in disappointment.
:::

## Saturday at the match

The story ends where it began — at the stadium on Saturday. The family watches United; **Danny Casey** takes a pass, beats the defenders, and **scores the second goal**; the crowd roars; her father is delighted; Sophie's hero is real and brilliant and utterly out of reach. She watches the goal as everyone does, from the stands. The real Danny Casey belongs to the whole crowd; the one who asked her for a date exists only in her head.

## Themes and the writer's message

- **Adolescent fantasy versus reality.** Sophie's dreams are the natural escapism of a teenager; the story shows their cost when they replace reality rather than motivate effort.
- **Hero worship.** The crush on a sports star is universal; Barton treats it with sympathy, not scorn.
- **Class and limited opportunity.** The biscuit factory waits for both girls; the small house and the tired mother show how little room Sophie's dreams have.
- **Family relationships.** A disbelieving father, a sympathetic yet sceptical brother, a sister who longs for her brother's world — the family is drawn with precision.
- **The persistence of hope.** Even at the canal, Sophie still sees him coming. That is both her weakness and her humanity.

**Style:** third-person narration close to Sophie's mind, with the daydreams written in the same tone as real events so that the reader shares her confusion; natural dialogue; an ironic title.

Characters: **Sophie** (dreamer), **Jansie** (realist), **Geoff** (silent, admired), **the father** (hearty, dismissive), **Derek**, **Frank**, **Danny Casey** (the hero who is only a name on the field).
