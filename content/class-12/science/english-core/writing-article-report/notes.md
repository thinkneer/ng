# Article and report — the 5-mark long composition

The last Section B question gives you a choice between an **article** (for a magazine or newspaper) and a **report** (of an event, for a school magazine or a newspaper). Both are 120–150 words, both carry 5 marks (format 1, content 2, expression 2), and both reward the same habit: a clear structure that the examiner can see at a glance. The difference is in purpose — an article *argues or explains* an issue; a report *tells what happened* at an event.

## The article: an opinion with a shape

An article discusses an issue — pollution, social media, women's safety, the value of sport, online classes, road rage — for general readers. You are expected to inform, give your view and suggest a way forward, in a tone that is thoughtful and slightly formal.

Format is light: a **title** (catchy, short, often a question or a phrase) and a **by-line** (*By Aditi Rao* or just your name/class). Then three or four paragraphs:

1. **Introduction** — hook the reader: a striking fact, a question, a short scene, or a general statement of the issue. State the problem in one sentence.
2. **Body (one or two paragraphs)** — causes and effects, or two sides of the issue, with examples. Concrete points: numbers, situations, consequences.
3. **Conclusion** — your view and two or three suggestions; end with a strong sentence that sticks (a call to action or a hopeful line).

:::skeleton Article — annotated model (shortened to show the shape)
**Screens Before Sleep: A Quiet Health Crisis** ← title
By Aditi Rao, Class XII ← by-line
*Ask any teenager when they last slept before midnight, and the answer is usually a laugh. Phones have quietly rewritten our nights.* ← hook + issue
*Doctors link late-night scrolling to poor sleep, falling grades and anxiety. The blue light of screens delays the sleep hormone, and the endless feed keeps the brain alert when it should be winding down. Many students report headaches and a constant feeling of tiredness.* ← body: causes, effects, examples
*The solution is not to throw the phone away but to put it away: a fixed screen-off hour, chargers kept outside the bedroom, and parents who model the same habit. Schools can help by teaching sleep as seriously as nutrition.* ← conclusion: view + suggestions
*A good night's sleep is the cheapest medicine we keep ignoring.* ← closing line
:::

:::tip Title and by-line are the format mark
An article without a title or without a by-line loses the format mark instantly. Write both before the first paragraph.
:::

## The report: what happened, told in order of importance

A report describes an event that has already taken place — an inter-school competition, an annual day, a science exhibition, a cleanliness drive, a road accident, a flood relief camp. It is **factual, in the past tense**, mostly **third person**, with the **passive voice** where natural (*The function was inaugurated by…*).

Format: a **headline** (short, in caps or bold), a **by-line** (*By a Staff Reporter* for a newspaper; your name and designation for a school magazine), and for a newspaper report a **place and date line** at the start of the first paragraph (*New Delhi, 14 November:*). Then the body follows the **inverted pyramid**: the most important facts first, details later.

1. **Opening paragraph** — the 5 Ws in two sentences: *what* happened, *who* organised/was involved, *when*, *where*, *why* (purpose).
2. **Details paragraph** — how the event unfolded in order: inauguration, chief guest, main activities, highlights, numbers (participants, schools, money raised), notable moments.
3. **Closing paragraph** — the outcome or significance: winners, prizes, response of the audience, a line from the organiser or chief guest (reported, not invented as a long quote), vote of thanks.

:::skeleton Report — annotated model (shortened to show the shape)
**SCIENCE EXHIBITION DRAWS RECORD CROWD** ← headline
By a Staff Reporter ← by-line
*Jaipur, 14 November:* ← place and date line
*A two-day science exhibition organised by Sunrise Public School concluded here yesterday, with more than sixty models from twenty schools on display.* ← 5 Ws
*The exhibition was inaugurated by the District Collector, who praised the students' focus on water conservation. Working models of solar desalination units, a low-cost air-quality sensor and a smart irrigation system drew the largest crowds. Over two thousand visitors, including parents and students from neighbouring schools, attended.* ← details in order, with numbers
*The first prize was won by a team from Modern School for their waste-segregation robot. The Principal thanked the participants and announced that the exhibition would be an annual event.* ← outcome, winners, closing
:::

:::tip Past tense, mostly passive
"The chief guest inaugurates the function" or "We had a lot of fun" break the report's voice. Write *was inaugurated, were displayed, were given away*; keep "I" out unless the question asks for a first-person report of an accident you witnessed.
:::

## Telling them apart in the question

- "Write an **article** on…", "for your school magazine expressing your views", "highlighting the problem and suggesting measures" → **article**: issue, opinion, suggestions, title and by-line.
- "Write a **report** on the … held in your school", "for a local daily", "as a staff reporter", "you witnessed…" → **report**: event, past tense, headline, by-line, place/date, 5 Ws.

A report never gives your opinion; an article is built around it.

## How the 5 marks are given

- **Format — 1 mark:** article: title + by-line; report: headline + by-line (+ place/date for newspapers).
- **Content — 2 marks:** article: the issue, causes/effects, suggestions, with examples; report: 5 Ws, sequence, highlights, outcome. Cover what the question lists.
- **Expression — 2 marks:** grammar, spelling, vocabulary, cohesion (linking words: *moreover, however, as a result, finally*), appropriate tone, 120–150 words.

Plan for ten minutes. Write a five-word margin outline (article: *hook – cause – effect – solution – close*; report: *what/when/where – who – highlights – result*), then write. Count roughly: three paragraphs of about 45 words each.

For the official pattern and a model question, see the CBSE 2026–27 sample paper on [cbseacademic.nic.in](https://cbseacademic.nic.in/).
