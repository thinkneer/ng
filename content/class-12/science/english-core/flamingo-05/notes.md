# Indigo — Louis Fischer

Text: NCERT *Flamingo*, Chapter 5 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)), an excerpt from Louis Fischer's biography *The Life of Mahatma Gandhi*. Fischer, an American journalist, lets Gandhi tell the story of **Champaran (1917)** — the first time Gandhi used **civil disobedience** in India, and the episode that, in Gandhi's own view, made him understand that Indians could be fearless. It is a chapter about **how a leader is made**: not by speeches but by going to the problem, gathering facts, refusing to leave, and then staying to build.

## Rajkumar Shukla brings Gandhi to Champaran

The story begins at the **Indian National Congress session at Lucknow, December 1916**. A poor, thin, **illiterate sharecropper** named **Rajkumar Shukla** from **Champaran**, a district in Bihar near Nepal, approaches Gandhi: the peasants are suffering under the landlords' injustice, come and see. Gandhi has appointments elsewhere — Cawnpore, Ahmedabad — but Shukla simply **follows him everywhere**, for weeks, and finally to Gandhi's ashram. His **resolute** persistence wins: Gandhi agrees to come to Champaran, and asks Shukla to meet him in Calcutta.

:::tip Shukla's quality
Questions ask what made Gandhi go to Champaran. The answer is Shukla's **tenacity** — an unlettered peasant who would not give up — rather than any argument. Mention that he followed Gandhi from Lucknow to Cawnpore, Ahmedabad and Calcutta.
:::

**In one breath:** an illiterate but stubborn sharecropper, Rajkumar Shukla, follows Gandhi across India until Gandhi agrees to visit Champaran.

## Patna and Muzzafarpur: lawyers and untouchability

From Calcutta the two travel to **Patna**, where Shukla takes Gandhi to the house of **Rajendra Prasad**, a lawyer (later the first President of India), who is away. The **servants**, seeing a peasant companion of Shukla, take Gandhi for another peasant — perhaps an **untouchable** — and do not let him draw water from the well lest a drop pollute it. Fischer's small detail says a lot about caste in 1917 and about Gandhi's appearance.

Gandhi decides to gather facts first and goes to **Muzzafarpur**, where **Professor J. B. Kripalani**, with a crowd of students, receives him at the station at midnight — even in a remote town Gandhi's name has reached. He stays with **Professor Malkani**, a government-school teacher brave enough to host an advocate of home rule. In Muzzafarpur he meets **lawyers** who have represented sharecroppers in court and collected large fees; Gandhi chides them: in such cases going to court is useless — the peasants are so crushed and **fear-stricken** that courts are no help; what they need is to be **free from fear**.

**In one breath:** at Patna caste prejudice mistakes Gandhi for an untouchable; at Muzzafarpur he tells the fee-collecting lawyers that courts cannot free the fear-stricken peasants.

## The sharecropping system

Most arable land in Champaran was divided into large estates owned by **Englishmen** and worked by **Indian tenants**. The chief commercial crop was **indigo**. The landlords compelled all tenants to plant **15 per cent** of their holdings with indigo and surrender the entire indigo harvest as **rent** — the long-term contract.

Then **synthetic indigo**, developed in **Germany**, made natural indigo unprofitable. The landlords, seeing this, wanted to release the peasants from the 15 per cent arrangement — but for a price: they demanded **compensation** for freeing them. Many illiterate peasants agreed and paid. Others resisted and engaged lawyers; the landlords hired thugs. Then the peasants learnt about synthetic indigo and realised they had been cheated: they wanted their money back. This is the dispute Gandhi walked into.

**In one breath:** English landlords forced tenants to grow indigo on 15% of their land as rent; when German synthetic indigo killed the market, they extracted compensation from the peasants to release them — a swindle the peasants wanted undone.

## Motihari: the first act of civil disobedience

Gandhi first calls on the secretary of the **British landlords' association**, who refuses information to an outsider, and then on the **British official commissioner** of Tirhut division, who bullies him and advises him to leave. Gandhi does not leave; he goes to **Motihari**, the capital of Champaran, where crowds of lawyers and peasants gather. He begins recording the peasants' grievances.

Hearing that a peasant has been maltreated in a nearby village, he sets off on an elephant to see for himself. On the way a police **superintendent's messenger** overtakes him and orders him back to town, where he is served an **official notice to quit Champaran** at once. Gandhi signs a receipt and writes on it that he will **disobey** the order.

He is summoned to court the next day. That night he telegraphs Rajendra Prasad to come with influential friends, sends instructions to the ashram and a full report to the Viceroy. In the morning the town of Motihari is black with **peasants**: thousands have gathered spontaneously around the courthouse. The **officials are powerless** without Gandhi's cooperation; he helps them regulate the crowd — polite and friendly, giving them concrete proof that their might, so far dreaded and unquestioned, could be challenged by Indians.

**In one breath:** ordered to leave Champaran, Gandhi refuses, and thousands of peasants surrounding the courthouse show the British that their authority can be defied.

## The trial that collapsed

In court the prosecutor asks for a postponement; Gandhi protests and reads a statement pleading **guilty**. He is caught, he says, in a **conflict of duties**: not to set a bad example as a law-breaker, and to render humanitarian and national service for which he has come. He has disregarded the order not from want of respect for lawful authority but in obedience to the **higher law of our being — the voice of conscience**. He asks for the penalty.

The magistrate announces he will pronounce sentence after a two-hour recess and asks Gandhi to furnish bail; Gandhi refuses; the magistrate releases him without bail. After the recess the judge says he will not deliver judgement for several days and allows Gandhi to remain at liberty. Rajendra Prasad, Brij Kishor Babu, Maulana Mazharul Huq and other prominent lawyers arrive. Gandhi asks them what they will do if he is sentenced to prison; at first they say they will go home. He asks: what about the injustice to the sharecroppers? They confer, and decide — ashamed of deserting the peasants when a stranger was ready to go to jail for them — that **they would follow him into jail**. Gandhi exclaims that the **battle of Champaran is won**.

Several days later Gandhi receives a letter: the **Lieutenant-Governor** has ordered the case **dropped**. **Civil disobedience** has triumphed — the first time in modern India.

**In one breath:** Gandhi pleads guilty by conscience, refuses bail, the lawyers vow to join him in jail, and the Lieutenant-Governor drops the case — civil disobedience's first victory in India.

## The inquiry and the 25 per cent

Gandhi and the lawyers then conduct a far-flung **inquiry**: depositions by about **ten thousand peasants**, documents collected, the whole area seething with activity. The landlords complain; Gandhi is summoned to the Lieutenant-Governor, **Sir Edward Gait**, and after four protracted interviews an **official commission of inquiry** is appointed, with Gandhi as the sole representative of the peasants.

The evidence is so overwhelming that the landlords agree in principle to **refund** the money. How much? They expect Gandhi to demand the whole amount; he asks for **50 per cent**. The landlords' representative offers **25 per cent**, and to everyone's surprise Gandhi **accepts**. He explains: the amount of the refund was less important than the fact that the landlords had been obliged to **surrender part of the money and, with it, part of their prestige**. Until then, the planters had behaved as lords above the law; now the peasant saw he had rights and defenders, and learnt **courage**. Events justified him: within a few years the British planters **abandoned their estates**, which reverted to the peasants, and indigo sharecropping disappeared.

:::tip Why 25% was enough
This is the chapter's most-asked long answer. Give the three reasons: (1) the money mattered less than the principle; (2) the landlords' surrender broke their prestige and the peasants' fear; (3) the result — within years the planters left and the system ended.
:::

**In one breath:** an official commission finds for the peasants; Gandhi accepts a 25% refund because the landlords' surrender of prestige, not the sum, was the victory — and the planters soon left.

## Staying on: schools, health, self-reliance

Gandhi does not leave after the legal win. He sees the **cultural and social backwardness** of the villages and appeals for teachers: **Mahadev Desai** and **Narhari Parikh** come with their wives; **Devdas**, Gandhi's youngest son, comes; **Kasturba** teaches the ashram rules on **personal cleanliness** and **community sanitation**. **Primary schools** are opened in **six villages**. Health conditions are miserable; **Dr Dev** volunteers for six months, with three medicines — castor oil, quinine and sulphur ointment. Meanwhile Gandhi keeps a long-distance watch on the ashram, asking in letters about accounts and even whether a gap in the latrine wall has been filled.

**In one breath:** after the victory Gandhi stays to open six schools, teach cleanliness and bring a doctor — politics tied to the practical uplift of the poor.

## The lesson of Champaran: self-reliance

Fischer ends with two reflections. First, the Champaran episode was a **turning point** in Gandhi's life: he declares that what he did was ordinary — he simply told the British they could not order him about in his own country. Second, **self-reliance**: when **Charles Freer Andrews**, an English pacifist and devoted follower, is about to leave, Gandhi's lawyer friends want him to stay and help. Gandhi opposes it: relying on an Englishman's support would show weakness; the cause is just and they must rely on themselves to win. A **free India** cannot be built on an Englishman as a prop. Fischer sums up the lesson: Gandhi's politics were intertwined with the day-to-day problems of millions; his loyalty was to living human beings, not abstractions.

**In one breath:** Champaran taught Gandhi — and India — fearlessness and self-reliance; he refused even a sympathetic Englishman's help so that the victory would be India's own.

## Themes and the writer's message

- **Leadership by presence and fact.** Gandhi goes to the spot, collects evidence and refuses to leave; he turns a legal dispute into a moral one.
- **Fearlessness.** The peasants' real problem was fear; the trial, the crowd and the lawyers' vow break it.
- **Civil disobedience.** Open, non-violent refusal of an unjust order, accepting the penalty — its first Indian success.
- **Justice over victory.** Accepting 25% shows wisdom: principle and dignity matter more than money.
- **Social work with politics.** Schools, sanitation and health are part of freedom.
- **Self-reliance.** No English prop; Indians must win their own battles.

Characters: **Gandhi** (central), **Rajkumar Shukla** (tenacious peasant), **Rajendra Prasad** and the lawyers (converted from fee-takers to companions in jail), **J. B. Kripalani**, **Kasturba**, **Dr Dev**, **Charles Freer Andrews**, **Sir Edward Gait**.

**In one breath:** Fischer shows how Gandhi turned one district's swindle into India's first lesson in fearless, self-reliant civil disobedience.
