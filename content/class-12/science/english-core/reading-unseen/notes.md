# The unseen passage — a method, not luck

Section A of the English Core paper is worth **22 marks**: a **12-mark unseen passage** (Q1) and a **10-mark case-based or factual passage** (Q2). Nothing here can be memorised in advance, which frightens students — and that is exactly why it rewards a **method**. The passage is new every year; the question types are not. Learn to recognise the types, read in the right order, and the 22 marks become the most predictable in the paper. This chapter covers Q1; the next covers Q2. Check the official 2026–27 sample paper on [cbseacademic.nic.in](https://cbseacademic.nic.in/) for the exact set of items.

## What Q1 looks like

The passage is a **discursive or narrative** piece of about **400–450 words** — an opinion piece, a reflection, a short account — followed by about **ten items** for 12 marks. In the recent pattern the items mix:

- **Multiple-choice** items on the writer's purpose, tone or meaning (1 mark each);
- **Short answers** in a phrase or sentence (1 mark each);
- **Inference / justification** items that ask you to pick a statement and give a reason, or to explain why the writer uses a word or an example (1–2 marks);
- **Vocabulary** items: find a word or phrase from the passage that means…, or the opposite of… (1 mark);
- **True / false** or **complete the sentence** items (1 mark);
- One **2-mark** item asking you to explain an idea in about 40 words or list two points.

The marks are small and many, so **speed and accuracy** matter more than depth: nobody wants a paragraph for a 1-mark item.

**In one breath:** Q1 is a 400–450-word passage with about ten short items — MCQ, short answer, inference, vocabulary, true/false — for 12 marks.

## Reading in two passes

Students lose time by reading the passage three or four times. Read it **twice**, each time for a different purpose.

**Pass 1 — skim (about 2 minutes).** Read the first paragraph fully, then the **first sentence of each paragraph**, then the last paragraph fully. You now know the **topic**, the writer's **stance** (for, against, reflective, humorous) and the **shape** of the argument. Give each paragraph a two-word label in the margin ("problem", "example", "cause", "solution").

**Pass 2 — questions first, then scan.** Read all the questions. Underline the key word in each (a name, a number, a term, "tone", "purpose", "infer"). Now go back to the passage and **scan** — move your eyes to find the key word or its synonym — and read carefully only the sentences around it. For most items the answer lies within two sentences of the keyword. Your margin labels tell you which paragraph to scan.

:::tip Never answer from memory of the passage
The commonest 1-mark loss is answering what you *think* the passage said. Every item has a location in the text; find it, put your finger on it, then write. The examiner's key is built from exact sentences.
:::

## Question types and how each is marked

- **Purpose / main idea MCQ.** Options usually include one too broad, one too narrow, one about a detail, one right. The right option is the one that the *whole* passage supports — check it against your paragraph labels.
- **Tone / attitude MCQ.** Decide from the adjectives and verbs the writer chooses: critical, nostalgic, humorous, persuasive, cautionary, matter-of-fact. Avoid "angry" or "sad" unless the text is strongly emotional.
- **Inference.** The answer is **not stated** but is the only sensible conclusion from what is stated. Write the inference and, if asked, the **evidence phrase** from the text. A 2-mark inference needs the point (1) and the justification (1).
- **"Why does the writer say / use…"** items. Explain the **function**: to give an example, to contrast, to emphasise, to make a statistic concrete, to appeal to the reader's experience.
- **Vocabulary.** Read the sentence containing the word; replace the word with each option and see which keeps the meaning. For "find a word that means…", go to the paragraph indicated and look for a word of the same **part of speech**.
- **True / false / complete the sentence.** Check the exact claim against the text; a statement that is *partly* true is false.
- **Short answer (1 mark).** A phrase or one sentence; copy the key words from the text, no extras.
- **Short answer (2 marks, ~40 words).** Two points or one point plus explanation; write two sentences.

:::tip Lift, don't rewrite
For 1-mark short answers it is fine — even wise — to lift the relevant phrase from the passage. Rewriting invites errors. Rewrite only for inference and 2-mark items, where you must show understanding.
:::

## The time plan

Section A should take about **30–35 minutes** of the three hours: **18–20 minutes** for Q1, **12–15 minutes** for Q2. Within Q1: 2 minutes skim, 1 minute reading the questions, 12–14 minutes answering, 1–2 minutes checking numbers and negatives (*not*, *except*, *least*). Do Section A **first**, while your mind is fresh and the passage is unfamiliar; do not leave it for the end.

:::skeleton Practice passage — The Quiet Return of the Public Library
(1) For most of the twentieth century the public library was the most democratic room in any town. It asked nothing of you except silence and the return of books on time. Then the internet arrived, and a chorus of commentators announced that the library was finished: why walk to a building to borrow information when it could be summoned to a screen in seconds? Councils cut budgets, branches closed, and the obituaries were written.

(2) The obituaries were premature. In the last decade visits to public libraries in many countries have risen, and the people walking through the doors are not the ones the commentators expected. They are teenagers who need a quiet table and reliable Wi-Fi, job-seekers printing applications, new parents at a rhyme-time session, elderly readers who come as much for conversation as for novels, and students who have discovered that a room full of other people working is the best cure for procrastination. The library, it turns out, was never only about books. It was about a place.

(3) Librarians saw this before anyone else. They reorganised shelves to make room for study desks and soft chairs, added charging points, started coding clubs and language exchanges, and lent out seeds, tools and even musical instruments. The rule of silence loosened. A good library today is a hum, not a hush — a mixture of low voices, keyboards and a child somewhere laughing at a picture book.

(4) None of this means the printed book is irrelevant. Loans of physical books have held steady, and in several cities they have grown, especially among readers under twenty-five, who report that paper helps them concentrate in a way that a screen, with its endless invitations to look elsewhere, does not. The e-book has found its own audience, but it has not displaced its ancestor; the two live side by side, like radio and television.

(5) The deeper reason for the library's survival is economic. It is one of the last indoor spaces in a city where a person can spend an entire day without paying for anything. Cafés expect a purchase; malls expect shopping; parks close when it rains. The library expects only that you behave reasonably. For a teenager from a crowded flat or a pensioner on a fixed income, that free, warm, well-lit room is not a luxury but a necessity.

(6) The threat to libraries has therefore never really been the internet. It has been the belief, held by people who have not visited one in years, that a library is a warehouse of books and that a warehouse can be digitised. The buildings that survive will be the ones their communities use loudly enough to be heard at budget time. The quiet room, in other words, needs to make some noise.
:::

The questions on this passage — with the method applied to each — are in **Practice**. Try them under a 20-minute clock.

**In one breath:** one original passage, ten items, a 20-minute clock — the only way to turn method into marks.
