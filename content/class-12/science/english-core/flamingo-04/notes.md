# The Rattrap — Selma Lagerlöf

Text: NCERT *Flamingo*, Chapter 4 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)). Selma Lagerlöf, a Swedish writer and the first woman to win the Nobel Prize for Literature, sets the story in the iron-mining districts of Sweden and tells it like a folk tale: a wandering peddler, a theft, a mistaken identity, a kind young woman, a Christmas gift and a letter. Underneath the simple plot is one idea that every question comes back to: **the world is a rattrap, and the only thing that frees a trapped person is another person's kindness.**

## The peddler and his theory

The main character has no name. He is a **vagabond** who walks the roads selling small **rattraps** of wire that he makes himself from material begged at stores and farms. The business does not pay, so he also **begs and steals** a little to survive. His clothes are rags, his cheeks sunken, hunger shines in his eyes; the world has never been kind to him.

One day, thinking about his rattraps, he has an idea that pleases him: **the whole world is nothing but a big rattrap.** It offers people **baits** — riches, joys, shelter, food, heat and clothing — exactly as a rattrap offers cheese and pork. The moment someone lets himself be tempted to touch the bait, the trap closes on him, and everything comes to an end. The thought cheers him because the world has treated him badly: it is a pleasure to think ill of it, and to picture people he knows caught in the trap.

:::tip Why the theory consoles him
A common 2-mark question. Answer: the world had been unkind to him, so imagining others caught in its trap gave him a bitter satisfaction and a sense of superiority — he, who had never got the bait, felt he was outside the trap.
:::

**In one breath:** a poor peddler of rattraps decides the world itself is a rattrap baiting people with comforts; the idea consoles a man the world has ill-treated.

## The crofter's hospitality — and the thirty kronor

One dark December evening he knocks at a little grey cottage by the road, expecting the usual sour faces, and is surprised: the owner, an **old crofter** without wife or child, is glad of company. He gives the peddler **hot porridge** and **tobacco**, plays **cards** (mjölis) with him till bedtime and talks generously about himself. In his prosperous days he had worked at the **Ramsjö Ironworks**; now his **cow** supports him — she gives milk for the creamery, and last month he received **thirty kronor**. Suspecting the stranger does not believe him, he shows the **three ten-kronor notes**, which he keeps in a **leather pouch** hanging on a nail by the window frame.

Next morning both get up early, the crofter milks the cow, and the peddler leaves. Half an hour later he returns, **smashes a window pane**, takes the thirty kronor, hangs the pouch back and walks away. The hospitality was real; the betrayal is complete.

**In one breath:** a lonely crofter feeds him, trusts him and shows him his thirty kronor — and the peddler comes back and steals it.

## Caught in the rattrap himself

With the stolen money he avoids the public highway and turns into the **forest**, expecting to come out on the other side. It is a big, confusing wood; the paths twist back; by evening he realises he has been walking in a circle and is **lost**. The thirty kronor in his pocket now feel like a trap, and he remembers his own theory: the forest, with its trees and paths, is an impenetrable **prison**, and **he is caught in the rattrap** — he has let himself be fooled by a bait. The thief understands his own metaphor for the first time, from the inside.

**In one breath:** lost in the forest with the stolen notes, he realises that he himself has taken the bait and is caught in the world's rattrap.

## Ramsjö Ironworks: the mistaken identity

Exhausted, he hears the thumping of a **hammer** and follows the sound to the **Ramsjö Ironworks**. In the forge, the **master smith** and his helper are at the furnace; they barely glance at the ragged stranger, who sits down by the fire — such intruders are common. Then the **ironmaster** himself, a prominent owner, comes in on his nightly round. In the dim light he sees the tall figure, peers closely and takes him for an old regimental comrade, **Captain von Stahle**, fallen on hard times. "Nils Olof", he calls him, and invites him home for Christmas.

The peddler is alarmed — a rich man's house, with a sheriff somewhere, is the last place for a thief with thirty stolen kronor. He **refuses**, saying he needs no help. He does not correct the mistake, however; he simply declines. The ironmaster goes home for the night.

**In one breath:** the ironmaster mistakes the ragged peddler for an old army comrade and invites him home; the frightened thief refuses but lets the mistake stand.

## Edla Willmansson

Half an hour later the ironmaster's daughter, **Edla Willmansson**, arrives with a valet carrying a fur coat. She is not pretty, but modest and shy, and she looks at the peddler with **compassion**. She notices at once that he is **afraid** — he looks as if he has stolen something or escaped from jail — but she speaks kindly: he need only stay for Christmas Eve, he is free to leave whenever he wishes, they have no guests and simply want to share the holiday with someone who has none. Her **friendly manner** disarms him and he agrees to go.

:::tip Why he goes with Edla but not with the ironmaster
The ironmaster offered a comrade's hospitality to a captain — an identity the peddler knew was false, with the fear of exposure attached. Edla offered a stranger plain kindness, with freedom to leave and no questions. Compassion persuaded where status could not.
:::

**In one breath:** Edla's gentle compassion, and her promise that he may leave whenever he wants, succeed where her father's hearty invitation failed.

## Christmas Eve: the truth comes out

At the manor the peddler is bathed, shaved and dressed in a good suit of the ironmaster's. In daylight, at the breakfast table, the ironmaster sees the man clearly — and realises his mistake. He is furious: the fellow has deceived him, and he must have known it; he will send for the **sheriff**. The peddler defends himself: he never pretended to be anyone, he said from the start that he did not want to come, and the whole world is a rattrap — the finer the bait, the quicker the fall. He asks only to put on his rags and go.

Here Edla intervenes with the story's moral centre. She says the man should **stay for Christmas Day**. She had invited him; she wants him to have **one day of peace** in the year, since all year round he is chased from place to place, hungry and cold, with no home. Her father gives way, muttering that the man will probably steal the silver. The peddler stays, eats, sleeps, and is given the ironmaster's old suit to keep — Edla says it is a Christmas present, and he may come back next year if he wishes.

**In one breath:** exposed at breakfast, the peddler is about to be handed to the sheriff; Edla insists he be given one day of peace and a Christmas, and her father relents.

## The gift and the letter

On Christmas morning father and daughter go to **church**, where they learn that one of the old crofters of the ironworks was robbed of thirty kronor by a man who sold rattraps. The ironmaster is sure they have been hosting the thief and that the silver will be gone. Edla is miserable. But at home the valet says the stranger has left — and left something: a small packet for Miss Willmansson, a **rattrap** containing **three ten-kronor notes** and a **letter**.

The letter, written in a large, uneven hand, thanks her for her kindness: she has treated him all day as if he were a captain, and so he wants to be nice to her in return — he does not want her to be embarrassed at Christmas by a thief. He asks that the thirty kronor be returned to the old man by the roadside. The rattrap is a Christmas present from a rat who would have been caught in the world's rattrap had he not been **raised to captain**, because in that way he got the power to clear himself. The letter is signed **Captain von Stahle**.

:::tip The signature is the point
The peddler signs as Captain von Stahle not because he is one but because being treated as one made him behave like one. The borrowed dignity became real; that is the meaning of being "raised to captain".
:::

**In one breath:** he leaves Edla a rattrap with the stolen money and a letter signed Captain von Stahle — kindness has freed the rat from the trap.

## Themes and the writer's message

- **The world as a rattrap.** The central metaphor: comforts and riches are baits; greed and want make us grab them and lose our freedom. The peddler tests the theory on himself when he steals.
- **Human goodness is contagious.** Edla's trust and compassion awaken the peddler's own dignity; the crofter's kindness alone did not — because the crofter trusted carelessly, while Edla trusted *knowingly*, seeing his fear.
- **Loneliness.** The crofter without family, the peddler without a home, the ironmaster and Edla with no guests at Christmas — everyone is alone, and company is what each one wants.
- **Mistaken identity and redemption.** A folk-tale device becomes a moral one: given a better self to live up to, a man lives up to it.
- **Appearance and reality.** The ironmaster is fooled by firelight; the "captain" is a thief; the thief turns out to be honourable.
- **Style:** fairy-tale narration, gentle **humour** (the ironmaster's grumbling about the silver), **irony** (the thief preaching the rattrap theory to his host), suspense and a satisfying twist. The characters are types — the vagabond, the generous host, the gruff rich man, the kind daughter — which gives the story its universal feel.

**In one breath:** Lagerlöf's message is that most people are good at heart and that compassion, not punishment, brings that goodness out — even in a man caught in the rattrap of the world.
