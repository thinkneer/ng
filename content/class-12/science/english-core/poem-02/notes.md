# Keeping Quiet — Pablo Neruda

Text: NCERT *Flamingo*, Poem 2 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)). Pablo Neruda (1904–1973), the Chilean poet and Nobel laureate, wrote this poem as an invitation: let the whole world stop, just once, for the length of a count to twelve. Read the poem from the textbook, stanza by stanza, alongside this guide; the questions almost always point to one stanza and ask what it means.

## The invitation: count to twelve and be still

The poem opens by asking everyone to **count to twelve** and then to **keep still**. Why twelve? It is the count of the **hours on a clock**, the **months of a year**, a complete cycle — a small, measurable unit of time that anyone can spare. The stillness Neruda asks for has two parts: **no speaking** (for once, let us not speak in any language) and **no movement** (stop moving our arms so much). Language divides us into nations and arguments; our restless arms do the world's work and the world's damage. For twelve seconds, both are to stop.

## The exotic moment

What would that pause be like? Neruda calls it an **exotic moment** — strange and rare, because it has never happened — **without rush** and **without engines**. For once everyone on earth would be **together**, united not by language or flag but by a **sudden strangeness**, the shared experience of silence. In that stillness, people would actually see one another.

**In one breath:** a universal pause would be a rare, engine-less moment in which all humanity, for once, feels together.

## Who would stop hurting whom

The poem then imagines the pause in concrete scenes. **Fishermen in the cold sea** would **stop harming whales** — the exploitation of nature halts. The **man gathering salt** would stop and **look at his hurt hands** — the exploitation of the worker, who has never had a moment to notice his own wounds, halts too. Both images show how our ceaseless activity hurts others and ourselves; only when we stop do we notice.

**In one breath:** in the pause the fisherman spares the whale and the salt-gatherer finally sees his own wounded hands — harm to nature and to the self both stop.

## The wars

Next come the makers of war: those who prepare **green wars**, **wars with gas**, **wars with fire**, and **victory with no survivors**. "Green wars" has two readings students should know: wars against the **environment** (destroying forests and the planet), or wars dressed up as fresh and new. Gas and fire are chemical and conventional weapons. The phrase about victory without survivors is the poem's sharpest irony: a war so total that the winner has no one left to rule. In the pause these men would put on **clean clothes** and **walk about with their brothers in the shade**, doing nothing — enemies turned into brothers resting together.

:::tip Victory with no survivors
Examiners ask what this phrase means. Answer: a war in which everyone dies, so that victory is meaningless; a warning against total (nuclear/chemical) war. Pair it with "clean clothes" — clean of blood, of guilt.
:::

## Not death, but life

Neruda anticipates an objection: is he asking for **total inactivity**, a world that simply stops? No. He says plainly that what he wants should not be confused with death — he wants no truck with death. **Life is what it is about**; the stillness is for the sake of a better life. The problem, he says, is that we are so **single-minded about keeping our lives moving** that we never stop to **understand ourselves**, and we end up **threatening ourselves with death**. The quiet he asks for is a pause for **introspection** — a chance to interrupt the sadness of never understanding ourselves.

## The Earth's lesson

How can stillness be alive? **The Earth can teach us.** In **winter everything seems dead** — bare trees, frozen ground — and yet in spring it all proves to be **alive**. Apparent stillness hides life getting ready. So a pause in human activity is not an end but a preparation, a season of rest before renewal.

**In one breath:** like the Earth, which seems dead in winter but lives again, a moment of human stillness can be the seed of new life.

## The closing

The poet counts to twelve again, asks everyone to keep quiet, and says that he will **go**. He has made his request; the rest is for us to do. The ending returns to the beginning, like the clock face coming back to twelve — a cycle completed.

## Poetic devices and form

- **Symbolism:** twelve (the clock, the year, completeness); engines (modern hurry); clean clothes (freedom from guilt and blood); shade (rest, peace); the Earth in winter (apparent death hiding life).
- **Imagery:** fishermen and whales, the salt-gatherer's hands, warriors in the shade — concrete pictures of abstract ideas.
- **Irony / paradox:** victory with no survivors; stillness that is not death but life.
- **Repetition:** the count to twelve, the call to keep quiet — a refrain-like frame.
- **Antithesis:** stillness vs movement, life vs death, war vs brotherhood.
- **Free verse**, conversational, direct address ("we", "us") that makes the reader a participant.
- **Tone:** gentle, persuasive, reflective — an invitation, not a command.

## Themes and the poet's message

- **Introspection:** we never pause to look inward; a moment of quiet could change us.
- **Peace and universal brotherhood:** silence unites where languages divide; enemies become brothers.
- **Environmental concern:** the whales, the green wars — humanity's harm to nature.
- **Critique of modern haste:** engines, rush, single-mindedness about keeping life moving.
- **Stillness as renewal, not death:** the Earth's example.

Neruda's message is practical and hopeful: a short, shared pause — to stop, to look at our hands, to see each other — might be enough to make us gentler with the planet and with one another.

**In one breath:** stop for a moment, understand yourself, see your brother, spare the earth — the quiet of the poem is the beginning of peace.
