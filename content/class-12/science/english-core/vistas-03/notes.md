# Journey to the End of the Earth — Tishani Doshi

Text: NCERT *Vistas*, Chapter 3 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)). Tishani Doshi is an Indian poet and writer; this is a piece of **travel writing with a purpose**. She joins an expedition to **Antarctica** and uses it to make a case: the coldest, emptiest continent is where you can read **the planet's past** and glimpse **its future** — and both tell us to change how we live. The examiner wants facts (the ship, Gondwana, the programme) and ideas (why go to Antarctica, what we learn, why students).

## The journey

Doshi travels on a **Russian research vessel**, the **Akademik Shokalskiy**, heading for the coldest, driest, windiest continent — Antarctica. Her journey begins in **Madras** (Chennai), **13.09 degrees north** of the Equator, and takes over **100 hours**: she crosses **nine time zones**, **six checkpoints**, **three bodies of water** and at least **three ecospheres**. She describes her first sight of the continent — its expansive **white landscape** and **uninterrupted blue horizon** — as relief mixed with wonder, and then a profounder question: how could there ever have been a time when India and Antarctica were part of the same landmass?

## Gondwana: the deep past

The answer to her question is **Gondwana**. About **650 million years ago** a giant southern supercontinent existed, centred roughly where Antarctica is now; India and Antarctica were part of the same landmass. There were no humans; the climate was warm, and flora and fauna flourished for about 500 million years. Then the **dinosaurs died out**, the age of mammals began, and the landmass **broke apart** into the countries we know, shaping the globe as it is today.

Why does this matter? Because to visit Antarctica is to understand where we have come from and where we could be going: the continent holds in its **ice-cores** half a million years of **carbon records**, and in its landscape the story of **continental drift**, of **evolution and extinction**, of the planet before us. It is a **pristine** place that has never had a human population — the only one where you can see Earth as it was.

:::tip Why Antarctica?
A standard 2-mark question. Answer: (1) to understand the Earth's past — Gondwana, drift, ice-core records of climate; (2) to see our present impact — warming, ozone; (3) to grasp our future — what happens if ice sheets melt. Add: it is pristine, with no human history.
:::

## Human history is a blink

Doshi puts human time against geological time. Humans have been on Earth for only about **12,000 years** of civilisation — a few seconds on the geological clock — yet in that short period we have created ruckus: wars, cities, population growth, and a battle with other species for limited resources. We have **burned fossil fuels**, putting a **blanket of carbon dioxide** around the planet and raising the global temperature. The ice sheets of **Antarctica**, which hold most of the planet's fresh water, are where the consequences will show first: Will the **West Antarctic ice sheet** melt entirely? Will the **Gulf Stream** be disrupted? Is the world heading for the end — as some predict?

## Students on Ice

Doshi travelled with **Students on Ice**, a programme run by the Canadian **Geoff Green**, who takes **high-school students** to the ends of the Earth. Green had grown tired of taking celebrities and retired, rich, curiosity-seekers who could give nothing back; he decided to take **young people** — the **future policy-makers** — because at their age they are ready to **absorb, learn and act**. The programme's success is simple: it is one thing to read about melting ice, and another to **see** glaciers retreating and ice shelves collapsing. Standing there, no one can remain unmoved; the students return as ambassadors for the planet.

**In one breath:** Geoff Green's Students on Ice takes future decision-makers to Antarctica because seeing the ice melt changes people as no textbook can.

## Phytoplankton: take care of the small things

Doshi's most important scientific point is about the smallest living things. The waters of the Southern Ocean are full of **phytoplankton** — single-celled plants, the **grasses of the sea** — which, through **photosynthesis**, nourish and sustain the entire **marine food chain**: krill, fish, seals, whales, birds. But phytoplankton depend on the **ozone layer**; a **depleted ozone layer** lets in more ultraviolet light, which harms them. Damage to phytoplankton would ripple up through every creature of the Southern Ocean and beyond, and would also affect the planet's **carbon cycle**, since phytoplankton absorb carbon dioxide.

The lesson she draws is a proverb: **take care of the small things and the big things will fall into place**. In ecology, the smallest organisms hold up the largest systems.

:::

## Walking on the ocean

The expedition's defining moment comes near the end: the ship is wedged between stretches of white, and the 52 members are asked to **climb down the gangplank and walk on the ocean**. Beneath their feet is a **metre-thick ice pack**, and beneath that, **180 metres of living, breathing salt water**. In the distance, **Crabeater seals** sun themselves on ice floes. Doshi feels the **beauty of balance** in play on the planet, and the question that ends the chapter: a million years from now, what will Antarctica look like — will it still be ice, or will it be **warm**? That question is not about Antarctica alone; it is about whether we act.

## Themes and the writer's message

- **Geological time vs human time:** Gondwana, 650 million years, against 12,000 years of civilisation that have changed the climate.
- **Climate change and responsibility:** the carbon blanket, melting ice sheets, the ozone layer; Antarctica as the planet's early-warning system.
- **Interdependence:** phytoplankton to whales — small things uphold the large.
- **Education and youth:** seeing is understanding; the young must be taken to the ice because they will decide the future.
- **Humility and wonder:** the human species is tiny and late on a planet that was fine without it.

**Style:** first-person travel narrative; facts and figures (100 hours, nine time zones, 650 million years, 12,000 years, 180 metres) woven into reflection; questions that turn the reader into a participant; vivid, quiet descriptions of white and blue.
