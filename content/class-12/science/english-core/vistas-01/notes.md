# The Third Level — Jack Finney

Text: NCERT *Vistas*, Chapter 1 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)). Jack Finney (1911–1995) was an American writer of science fiction and fantasy who loved one idea above all: that **time is not a straight line** and a person might slip into the past. "The Third Level" is a short, clever story told by a man who may or may not have done exactly that. Read it for the plot first, then for the question it asks: *why would a modern man want to escape to 1894?*

## Charley and the station with two levels

The narrator, **Charley**, is a thirty-one-year-old New Yorker. He begins by telling us that the presidents of the New York Central and the New York, New Haven and Hartford railroads will swear that **Grand Central Station** has only **two levels** — but he has been on the **third**. His friends think it is a **waking-dream wish fulfilment**; his psychiatrist friend **Sam** says he is **unhappy**, that the modern world is full of **insecurity, fear, war and worry**, and that Charley wants to **escape**. Charley's wife **Louisa** does not like that at all, and Sam hastily adds that everybody wants to escape — stamp collecting, for instance, is a "temporary refuge from reality". Charley's grandfather started the collection, and Charley points out that in his grandfather's days life was calm, so he did not need a refuge — a joke that quietly supports Sam's point.

**In one breath:** Charley claims to have found a third level at Grand Central; Sam the psychiatrist calls it an escape from an insecure modern world, and ties even stamp collecting to the same wish.

## How Charley reached the third level

One night, working late, Charley decides to take the **subway** home from Grand Central. He takes the stairs to the first level, goes down to the second where the suburban trains leave, ducks into an **arched doorway** towards the subway — and gets lost. Grand Central, he says, has been growing like a tree, pushing out new **corridors and staircases** like roots; he has often got into tunnels he never knew existed and suspects there is a corridor somewhere that leads into the past. This time the corridor turns left, slopes downward, and he comes out on a **third level**.

The place is different. The room is **smaller**, with fewer ticket windows and train gates; the information booth is **wooden** and old-looking; the man in it wears a **green eyeshade** and long black **sleeve-protectors**; the lights are **open-flame gaslights**; there are **brass spittoons** on the floor. The people are dressed in 1890s fashion — a man with a **derby hat**, a black four-button suit with tiny lapels and a big black handlebar **moustache**; a woman in a dress with leg-of-mutton sleeves and skirts to the top of her high-buttoned shoes. Charley sees a **locomotive** with a funnel-shaped stack. And he glances at a newsstand: the paper is **The World** — which had not been published for years — and the date on it is **June 11, 1894**.

## Galesburg, and why Charley wanted to go

Charley at once wants **two tickets to Galesburg, Illinois** — for himself and Louisa. Galesburg is the town where he was born, and he describes the Galesburg of 1894 from his grandfather's time: a wonderful town with big old frame houses, huge lawns and tremendous trees meeting over the streets; **summer evenings** twice as long, people sitting on their lawns, men smoking cigars, women waving palm-leaf fans, with fireflies all around, in a **peaceful world**. The First World War was still twenty years off and the Second forty. That is the whole of his wish: to live where there is no war, no fear, no hurry.

He asks for the tickets; the clerk tells him the fare. Charley hands over modern money — and the clerk stares at the bills and accuses him of trying to **cheat** him: these are not **old-style currency** — the big bills of 1894 — and he threatens to have Charley arrested. Charley hurries back the way he came, into the corridor, and up to the present.

**In one breath:** Charley tries to buy two tickets to the peaceful Galesburg of 1894, but his modern money is taken for a swindle and he flees.

## The hunt for the third level — and old-style money

Charley is determined to go back. The next day he **withdraws almost all his savings** — about three hundred dollars — and buys **old-style currency** from a coin dealer, which costs him a premium: he gets less than two hundred dollars' worth of 1894 money. (Money of that period, he notes, buys a great deal more, so it is worth it.) But he has **never found the corridor again**. Louisa worries and refuses to let him search alone; so he stops looking, officially. Sam's diagnosis seems confirmed: there is no third level, only a wish.

**In one breath:** Charley converts his savings to 1894 currency but can never find the corridor again — Louisa is anxious and Sam seems right.

## The first-day cover: proof, or more fantasy?

Then comes the twist that gives the story its shape. **Sam disappears**. Nobody knows where he has gone. And one day, in Charley's grandfather's **stamp collection**, Charley finds a **first-day cover** that should not be there. A first-day cover is an envelope with a new stamp, posted on the day the stamp is issued, usually addressed to the collector himself and never opened; the collection has many, but this one bears a letter inside — and it was never there before.

The envelope is addressed to Charley's **grandfather** at his old Galesburg address; the stamp is a six-cent, dull brown, with a picture of President **Garfield**; the postmark is **Galesburg, Illinois, July 18, 1894**. Inside is a note, in Sam's handwriting, saying he found the third level, has been in Galesburg two weeks, that it is all Charley said — quiet, with people sitting on lawns through long evenings and the band playing in the park, that Charley and Louisa should keep looking until they find it, and that it is worth it. It is signed **Sam**.

And Charley adds the last piece: at the stamp and coin store he learns that Sam bought **eight hundred dollars' worth of old-style currency**. That, Charley says, should set Sam up in a nice little **hay, feed and grain business** — which was always what he said he wanted. He certainly could not go back to his old business — not in Galesburg, Illinois, in 1894. Because, Charley reminds us, **Sam was a psychiatrist**.

:::tip Why the ending is funny
The last line is the joke the examiner wants you to catch: a psychiatrist has no customers in a town where nobody is anxious. The man who diagnosed Charley's escape wish has escaped himself.
:::

## Fantasy or reality? Reading the story two ways

The story is deliberately **ambiguous**, and good answers show that:

- **It is fantasy / psychological:** Sam's diagnosis fits — Charley is unhappy and stressed; Grand Central's confusing corridors invite a daydream; he can never find the level again; the letter could be Charley's own invention, a wish-fulfilment carried one step further (notice that the first-day cover was addressed to his grandfather, which a stamp-collector's imagination could supply).
- **It is real / time travel:** the physical details are exact; the clerk's reaction to the money is not the kind of thing one dreams; the first-day cover is a physical object; Sam has in fact disappeared, and the coin dealer confirms his purchase of old currency.

Finney leaves both doors open. What matters thematically is not whether the level exists but **what it stands for**.

## Themes and the writer's craft

- **Escape from modern insecurity:** the central theme. War, worry, fear and hurry make the past look like paradise; the third level is the human wish to go back to a simpler time.
- **Intersection of time and space:** Finney's favourite idea — the past is not gone but lies alongside the present, reachable through a wrong turning.
- **Nostalgia and its limits:** Charley's Galesburg is his grandfather's memory, idealised; the story gently shows how we romanticise a past we never lived.
- **Fantasy and reality:** the blurred boundary, the unreliable narrator.
- **Hobbies as refuge:** stamp collecting as a "temporary refuge", which becomes, through the first-day cover, the bridge to the past.

**Craft:** first-person conversational narration; precise period detail that makes the impossible believable; a frame (Charley addressing us, anticipating disbelief); irony and a twist ending that turns the sceptic into the escapee.

Characters: **Charley** (narrator, stressed, nostalgic, a stamp collector), **Louisa** (his anxious wife), **Sam** (psychiatrist friend who explains and then escapes), the **clerk** of 1894.
