# Poets and Pancakes — Asokamitran

Text: NCERT *Flamingo*, Chapter 6 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)), from Asokamitran's memoir *My Years with Boss*. The author worked at **Gemini Studios, Madras**, one of the biggest film studios of the 1940s–50s, run by **S. S. Vasan** (the "Boss"). This chapter is not a story with a plot; it is a set of **sketches** — the make-up room, the office boy, Subbu, the lawyer, the visiting foreigners — held together by **gentle humour** and **irony**. The examiner wants you to know the people, the incidents and, above all, the *tone*: affectionate mockery of a world that took itself seriously.

## Pancake and the make-up department

**Pancake** was the brand name of the **make-up material** Gemini Studios bought in truckloads; famous actresses of the day must have used it. The make-up department was housed in what was once **Robert Clive's stables** — one of the many buildings in Madras linked to Clive, who, the author jokes, seems to have spent his short life either in Madras or on the sea.

The make-up room looked like a hair-cutting salon with large mirrors and **incandescent lights** at all angles, which made the heat unbearable. The author's point: the department's job was to turn any decent-looking person into a hideous **crimson-hued monster** with the help of truckloads of Pancake and lotions, so that the person would look presentable under the fierce lights in the studio.

The department was an example of **national integration** long before the term existed: it was first headed by a **Bengali**, then by a **Maharashtrian**, assisted by a **Dharwar Kannadiga**, an **Andhra**, a **Madras Indian Christian**, an **Anglo-Burmese** and the usual local **Tamils**. There was a strict **hierarchy**: the chief make-up man did the main actors and actresses, his senior assistant the second hero and heroine, the junior assistant the main comedian, and so on; the **crowd players** were handled by the **office boy**.

:::tip Two things about the make-up room
Questions ask (1) why it was an example of national integration — list the regions; (2) what it did — made good-looking people look ugly for the camera. Both are 2-mark staples.
:::

**In one breath:** Pancake was the make-up brand; the department in Clive's old stables, staffed from across India, turned decent faces into crimson monsters under hot lights.

## The office boy — a frustrated poet

The "office boy" was in fact a man of **forty**, who joined the studios hoping to become a star actor, a screenwriter, director or lyricist, and ended up slapping paint on the faces of **crowd players** on crowd-shooting days, using a mixing pot and a thick brush. He was a **bit of a poet**, and he was convinced that his talent was being wasted — a frustration he blamed on **Kothamangalam Subbu**.

The office boy's grievance brings in the author's own job. Asokamitran sat in a cubicle **cutting out newspaper clippings** on a wide variety of subjects and pasting them in files; to most people he seemed to be doing nothing, so everyone felt free to come and give him advice — the office boy most of all. Whenever he saw the author idle, he would come in and pour out his epics of frustration about Subbu. The author's dry comment is that the office boy's barbs were aimed at Subbu, but the author himself was the one who had to hear them.

**In one breath:** a forty-year-old "office boy" who painted crowd faces blamed Subbu for his failed poetic career and vented it on the author, whose clippings job looked like idleness.

## Kothamangalam Subbu

**Subbu** was officially in the **Story Department** but was in effect the **No. 2** at Gemini Studios. He had his limits in formal education, but he was a **Brahmin** — a virtue, the author says, that gave him exposure to more affluent situations and people — and, above all, he had the gift of **loyalty**: he identified himself completely with his principal, the Boss, and turned all his energy to the Boss's benefit.

He was tailor-made for films. Give him a scene and he would come up with **four ways of doing it**; if the producer did not like them, he would offer **fourteen** more. His inventiveness made filmmaking look easy. He was also a **poet** who chose to write for the masses, a **novelist** (the author mentions his novel *Thillana Mohanambal* and its unforgettable characters), and a brilliant **actor** — never the hero, but a supporting player who often outshone the lead. He was **generous**, feeding and housing dozens of near and dear ones, and he was **charitable** and improvident. Yet he had **enemies**, probably because of his success and his closeness to the Boss, which explains the office boy's resentment. Subbu had no personal grudges even towards those who disliked him.

:::tip Balanced portrait
When asked to describe Subbu, give the full picture: loyalty to the Boss, versatility (four ways, fourteen more), poet-novelist-actor, generosity — and the author's hint that his position made him hated. A one-sided "great man" answer loses the irony mark.
:::

**In one breath:** Subbu, the Boss's loyal No. 2, was an inventive filmmaker, poet, novelist, actor and generous host — and, being so successful, the target of envy.

## The legal adviser — "the opposite"

The Story Department also had a **lawyer**, officially the **legal adviser**, whom everyone called **the opposite**. Among the poets and dreamers of the department he was the one man of logic. He wore **pants, a tie and sometimes a coat** while everyone else wore **khadi dhotis** and loose shirts; he looked alone and helpless, a man of cold logic in a crowd of **dreamers**.

He is remembered for one incident. A **temperamental actress** — extremely talented but given to outbursts — blew up on the set one day; the lawyer quietly **switched on the recording equipment** and, when she paused, played her tirade back to her. She was so shocked that she lost her voice and her nerve, and **her career ended**. The author adds, in his sly way, that the lawyer probably did it without any malice — just the lawyer's instinct for evidence — but it finished a career. When the Story Department was closed, he lost his job too — the author compares him to a dog that has lost its tail, and admits he does not quite see the connection himself.

**In one breath:** the lawyer, the only logical man among the poets, in pants and tie, ended a temperamental actress's career by recording her outburst — a tale of logic misused in a world of feeling.

## Khadi, Congress and the fear of communism

Gemini Studios was the favourite haunt of **poets** who wore khadi and worshipped **Gandhi**; beyond that they had no political affiliation, except that they were all **against communism**. In their minds a communist was a godless man without a wife or children, who would kill his own parents and spread unrest; this half-understood fear sets up the two visits that follow.

**In one breath:** the studio's poets were Gandhian, khadi-clad and vaguely but firmly anti-communist.

## The Moral Re-Armament Army

In **1952** a group of **two hundred** people from twenty nationalities — the **Moral Re-Armament Army (MRA)**, led by **Frank Buchman** — visited Madras and staged two plays at Gemini, **Jotham Valley** and **The Forgotten Factor**, several times. The MRA was a kind of **counter-movement to international communism**, and the big bosses of Madras, like Vasan, simply **played into their hands**. The studio folk could hardly follow the plays' simple homilies, but they loved the **sets and costumes** — above all the **sunrise and sunset** scenes done with bare stage, a white background and a tune. For years afterwards, almost every Tamil play had a scene of sunrise and sunset in the MRA manner. The author's humour: a serious political mission was remembered in Madras for its lighting effects.

**In one breath:** the anti-communist MRA's plays impressed Gemini mostly by their sunrise–sunset stage effects, which Tamil drama copied for years.

## The Englishman nobody understood

A few months later another visitor arrived: an **English poet**, editor of a British periodical. Gemini had never had anything to do with English poetry; nobody understood why he had come, and a poet addressing an audience of Tamil film workers made no sense. The Boss welcomed him with a long speech; the visitor spoke, but his **accent** was so strong that nobody understood a word of what he said about the thrills of poetry or of a free world. The guest was as bewildered by the audience as the audience by him. For years the author could not even remember his name.

**In one breath:** an English poet lectured Tamil film workers, who understood nothing of his accent or his purpose; both sides were baffled.

## The God That Failed — the mystery solved

Years later, having left the studios, the author bought a cheap pile of books from a pavement bookseller outside the Madras bookshop, among them **The God That Failed** — six eminent men of letters (Andre Gide, Richard Wright, Ignazio Silone, Arthur Koestler, Louis Fischer and **Stephen Spender**) describing their journeys **into communism and their disillusioned return**. The name on the cover jolted him: **Stephen Spender** — the editor of **The Encounter**, the English poet who had visited Gemini. The visit finally made sense: Gemini's Boss, who distrusted communism, had invited a famous **ex-communist** to speak — and nobody had known who he was.

The chapter's last irony is quiet: the author had once seen an ad for a short-story contest in *The Encounter* and wondered about its editor; the book revealed that the editor was the man whose speech no one at Gemini understood. Gemini had entertained a world-famous poet and failed to notice.

:::tip The connection the examiner wants
Spender was (1) the editor of *The Encounter*, (2) the poet who visited Gemini, and (3) one of the six writers in *The God That Failed*, which explains why an anti-communist Boss invited him. Answers that give only "the English poet" miss two marks' worth.
:::

**In one breath:** *The God That Failed* reveals the visitor as Stephen Spender, an ex-communist poet — and explains why the anti-communist Boss invited him.

## Themes, tone and the writer's method

- **Satire of the film world:** make-up that makes people ugly, a legal adviser among poets, a studio that hosts a poet it cannot understand.
- **Human nature:** envy (office boy), loyalty and talent (Subbu), cold logic (the lawyer), herd ideology (anti-communism without knowledge).
- **Irony** as the master device: the "office boy" of forty; "national integration" in a make-up room; the MRA remembered for sunsets; Spender unrecognised.
- **Style:** first-person memoir, digressive, understated, with deadpan humour and self-mockery (his own job of cutting clippings).

**In one breath:** Asokamitran's chapter is affectionate satire — a studio full of poets, politics and Pancake, seen by a man whose job was to look idle.
