# On the Face of It — Susan Hill

Text: NCERT *Vistas*, Chapter 5 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)). This is the only **play** in your syllabus — two scenes, two characters, one garden. Susan Hill (born 1942), an English novelist and playwright, wrote it as a radio play, so everything is in the **dialogue**: how **Derry**, a boy with a burnt face, speaks at the beginning and how he speaks at the end is the whole story. The title is a pun: *on the face of it* means "judging by appearances" — exactly what the play asks us not to do.

## Scene 1: a boy climbs into a garden

**Derry**, fourteen, climbs over the wall into a garden, thinking it is empty. It is not. **Mr Lamb**, an old man with a **tin leg**, is sitting there, and he is not angry — he says the **gate is always open**, Derry could have walked in. Derry wants to leave at once; he is used to being stared at and expects to be sent away. One side of his face was **burnt by acid**; he describes it as terrible, and he has heard people say so.

Mr Lamb refuses to be shocked. He has his own disability — children call him **Lamey-Lamb** because of the leg, which was blown off in the war — and he has decided long ago how to live with it: in a garden with the gate open, bees humming, **crab apples** ripening, and anyone welcome. He tells Derry to **sit down, listen to the bees** (Derry says they buzz; Mr Lamb says they *sing*), and later to help him pick the apples.

**In one breath:** Derry, a burnt-faced boy expecting rejection, climbs into Mr Lamb's garden and meets an old man with a tin leg who welcomes him and refuses to see a monster.

## Derry's bitterness

Derry talks the way years of stares have taught him. People look at his face and look away; he has heard a woman on a bus whisper to another that it is a terrible thing and only a mother could love that face; even his **mother**, he says, kisses him on the **other side**. He has heard them call him *poor boy* and tell him there are worse things — people in wheelchairs, the blind — but he does not want pity or comparisons. He hates **"Beauty and the Beast"**: in that story the beast turned into a prince when someone loved him; nobody, Derry says, will ever kiss his face. He says he does not **like being with people**, prefers to be **alone**, and expects nothing from life.

The dialogue shows **self-pity**, **withdrawal** and a sharp intelligence turned against himself. Mr Lamb's task is not to deny the face but to change what Derry does with it.

:::tip Quote Derry's complaints, not his words
Questions on Derry's attitude want the content — stares, whispers, mother kissing the other side, hatred of pity, Beauty and the Beast, wanting to be alone — in your words. One short phrase at most.
:::

**In one breath:** Derry is bitter, withdrawn and allergic to pity; he believes his face has decided his whole life.

## Mr Lamb's answers

Mr Lamb never argues head-on. He answers Derry's bitterness with **images and stories**:

- **Weeds and flowers.** Derry calls some plants weeds; Mr Lamb says he calls them all **growing things** — a weed is just a flower someone has decided not to like. The point: labels like *ugly* and *beautiful* are choices, not facts.
- **The man who was afraid of everything.** Mr Lamb tells of a man so frightened of being hurt — a bus might crash, a man might breathe germs on him — that he **locked himself in his room**, and then **a picture fell off the wall onto his head and killed him**. Hiding from life does not make you safe; it only makes you alone.
- **Everyone has something.** Some people have burnt faces, some tin legs, some are frightened of the dark; and everybody, in the end, is **the same inside**. Derry's face is not the whole of Derry.
- **Looking and listening.** Mr Lamb's advice is to **watch, listen, think** — to see the world instead of watching people watch you. Derry, he says, has two arms, two legs, eyes, ears, a brain — he can do what anyone can, and better, if he has the will.
- **His own loneliness.** When Derry asks whether Mr Lamb has friends, the old man dodges: people come in, some sit, some talk; children taunt him, tin leg and all. He has no family. We understand that his cheerfulness is a **choice made daily**, not a gift — which is why it can be taught.

Mr Lamb also asks Derry what he **hates**: Derry hates his face and the people who stare; Mr Lamb says hating can burn you worse than acid. The one sentence he hopes Derry will hear.

:::tip Mr Lamb's method in one line
He does not pity, does not compare and does not preach; he offers the garden, the bees, the apples, a story and a few questions — and leaves Derry to draw his own conclusions. That respect is what Derry has never been given.
:::

## Derry goes home — and comes back

Derry begins to change. He says he wants to come back to help with the apples, but first he must go home to tell his **mother** — three miles away — or she will worry. Mr Lamb notes gently that Derry may not return once he leaves: people say they will come back and do not. Derry insists he will.

**Scene 2** is Derry's home. His mother has heard of Mr Lamb and **forbids** Derry to go back: she has been told things about the old man. Derry argues: what he looks like is not important, and he has learnt more in that garden than anywhere; if he does not go now he will never go anywhere. He tells his mother that he wants to **be with people, do things** — the opposite of what he said an hour earlier — and he **runs back to the garden**.

**In one breath:** Derry goes home and, against his mother's ban, declares he wants to live in the world and runs back to Mr Lamb's garden.

## The ending

In the garden Mr Lamb has climbed a **ladder** to pick the crab apples. The ladder slips; he **falls** and lies still. Derry arrives, calls to him, kneels beside him and **weeps**. The play ends there.

The ending is deliberately open and painful. Mr Lamb, who was always ready for anyone who came, is not there when Derry finally comes. Yet Derry has come back — the gate was open, and he walked through it. The old man's lesson has landed in the one place that matters: Derry chose people over hiding. Whether Mr Lamb is dead or only injured (the play strongly suggests death), the boy who said he wanted to be alone is kneeling beside another human being, caring.

:::tip How to write about the ending
Give both halves: the tragedy (Mr Lamb falls just as Derry returns; the only person who accepted him is lost) and the hope (Derry has changed — he came back, he wants to live among people). Note the irony that Mr Lamb's cheerful "anyone may come" ends in his falling alone.
:::

## Themes and the playwright's craft

- **Appearance and reality:** the title; the face is not the person; weeds are flowers.
- **Disability and society's response:** stares, whispers, pity, taunts; how the disabled internalise them.
- **Isolation vs engagement:** the man who locked himself in; Derry's wish to be alone; Mr Lamb's open gate.
- **Hatred as the real wound:** acid burnt the face; hate can burn the self.
- **Loneliness of the kind:** Mr Lamb's own friendless life; kindness is a discipline.
- **Change through acceptance:** one afternoon of being treated as a person undoes years of being treated as a face.

**Craft:** two scenes, two voices, a garden setting rich in symbols (open gate, bees, crab apples, ladder); the dialogue carries the change in Derry from short bitter sentences to long eager ones; an abrupt, open ending.

Characters: **Derry** (bitter, intelligent, lonely, finally hopeful) and **Mr Lamb** (cheerful by choice, wise, lonely, generous); Derry's **mother** (protective, prejudiced by hearsay) appears in Scene 2.
