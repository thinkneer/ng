# The Last Lesson — Alphonse Daudet

The text is in NCERT *Flamingo*, Chapter 1 ([ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook.php)). Read the story once before this guide; here we talk through what happens, why it matters and how the examiner wants you to write about it.

## The setting: Alsace, 1870–71

The story is set in a village in **Alsace**, a French province on the border with Germany. France has just lost the **Franco-Prussian War**, and Alsace and Lorraine have passed into Prussian (German) hands. One morning an **order from Berlin** reaches the village: from now on only **German** will be taught in the schools of Alsace and Lorraine. The French teacher, **M. Hamel**, who has taught in the village for **forty years**, is to leave the next day. So today is the last French lesson.

Daudet, who was French, wrote this soon after the war. The story is his quiet protest: when a conqueror takes your land, the deepest loss is not the territory but the **language**, because language carries who you are.

**In one breath:** Alsace has been taken by Prussia; an order says only German will be taught; M. Hamel's forty years end with one last French lesson.

## Franz on the way to school

The narrator is **Franz**, a schoolboy who would rather be anywhere than in class. He is late, he has not learnt his lesson on **participles**, and M. Hamel has said he will question the class on it. The morning is warm and bright; birds are chirping at the edge of the woods and Prussian soldiers are drilling in the open field behind the sawmill. All of it tempts him to play truant, but he resists and runs to school.

On the way he passes the **bulletin board** outside the town hall, where for two years all the bad news has been posted: lost battles, the draft, orders from the commanding officer. A crowd is reading it. The blacksmith **Wachter** calls out to Franz not to hurry, that he will reach school in plenty of time — Franz thinks he is being made fun of, but the remark is a sad joke: there is no hurry any more, because French school is ending.

**In one breath:** Franz, late and unprepared, is tempted to skip school; the bulletin board and Wachter's remark hint that something has changed.

## The strange quiet of the classroom

Normally the school is noisy at the start of the day — desks opening and closing, lessons repeated aloud, the teacher's **ruler** rapping the table. Franz had planned to slip in under cover of that noise. Today everything is as still as a Sunday morning. Through the window he sees his classmates already in their seats and M. Hamel walking up and down with the iron ruler under his arm. He has to open the door and enter in front of everyone, blushing and frightened.

But M. Hamel is gentle: he tells Franz kindly to take his seat. Then Franz notices the unusual things. The teacher is wearing his **fine Sunday clothes** — the beautiful green coat, the frilled shirt, the embroidered black silk cap — which he wears only on inspection and prize days. And on the **back benches**, which are usually empty, sit **village people**: old **Hauser** with his three-cornered hat, the former mayor, the former postmaster and others, all looking sad. Hauser has brought an old, torn primer and holds it open on his knees with his big spectacles across the pages.

**In one breath:** the room is silent, M. Hamel is in his best clothes, and the villagers sit at the back — the signs of an occasion Franz does not yet understand.

## The announcement

M. Hamel climbs into his chair and tells the children, in the same grave and gentle voice, that this is the **last lesson** he will give them. The order has come from Berlin; the new master arrives tomorrow; today they must be very attentive.

The words hit Franz like a thunderclap. So *this* is what they had put up at the town hall. His last French lesson — and he can barely write! He will never learn any more. Suddenly he is sorry for every lesson he skipped to hunt for birds' eggs or slide on the Saar. The books he found such a nuisance a moment ago — his grammar, his history of the saints — now seem like **old friends** he cannot give up. And M. Hamel, whose ruler he feared, is about to leave for ever; Franz forgets all about the ruler and the crabby teacher he thought he knew.

Now Franz understands the villagers too. They have come to thank the master for his forty years of faithful service and to show their respect for the country that is no longer theirs. They are also sorry, like Franz, that they had not gone to school more.

**In one breath:** the news that this is the last French lesson turns Franz's boredom into regret and his fear of M. Hamel into affection; the villagers have come to pay their respects.

## Franz is questioned — and M. Hamel blames everyone, himself included

Franz's name is called to recite the rule for participles. He gets mixed up on the first words and stands there, heart beating, not daring to look up. M. Hamel does not scold him. Instead he says something that is the heart of the story: every day the people of Alsace said, "there is time enough, I'll learn tomorrow" — and now look where it has brought them. The Prussians can now mock them: you claim to be French and you cannot speak or write your own language.

Then M. Hamel turns the blame around. He blames the **parents**, who preferred to send their children to work on farms or in the mills for a few extra sous. He blames **himself**: he sent the boys to water his flowers instead of teaching them, and gave them a holiday when he wanted to go fishing. Nobody is spared, and nobody is humiliated — this is a teacher looking at a whole community's **procrastination** honestly.

:::tip "Procrastination" is the word examiners expect
When a question asks what Franz regrets or what M. Hamel blames the people for, use the term **procrastination** — putting off till tomorrow what should be done today — and give the two examples (parents sending children to work, Hamel's own fishing holidays).
:::

**In one breath:** Franz fails his recitation; M. Hamel blames Alsace's habit of putting things off — parents, children and himself alike.

## The lesson on language

M. Hamel then speaks about the **French language**: that it is the most beautiful, the clearest, the most logical language in the world, that they must guard it and never forget it, because when a people are enslaved, as long as they hold fast to their language, it is as if they held the **key to their prison**. This is the sentence students most need to understand: language is **identity** and **freedom**; a conquered people who keep their language keep a way out.

Then he opens the grammar and reads. Franz is amazed at how well he understands — everything seems easy. He feels that M. Hamel has never explained with so much patience, and that the poor man wants to give them everything he knows before he goes.

**In one breath:** M. Hamel's message — hold on to your language, it is the key to your prison; Franz understands his lesson perfectly for the first time.

## Writing, the pigeons and the little class

After grammar comes writing. M. Hamel has prepared new copies with the words **France, Alsace** written in a beautiful round hand, like little flags floating from the desks. Everyone works in silence; only the scratching of pens is heard. Beetles fly in, but nobody notices — even the smallest children trace their fish-hooks as if that too were French. Pigeons coo on the roof, and Franz wonders whether the Prussians will make even them **sing in German** — a child's bitter thought that captures the absurdity of forcing a language on a people.

Franz looks at M. Hamel sitting motionless, gazing at everything in the room as if he wants to fix it in his mind: the garden outside, the walnut trees he planted, the hopvine he trained over the windows. His sister is packing upstairs; tomorrow they leave the country for ever. Yet he has the courage to hear every lesson to the end. Then the little ones chant their **ba, be, bi, bo, bu**, and old Hauser at the back, spectacles on, spells the letters with them in a trembling voice — a scene that makes Franz want to laugh and cry at once.

**In one breath:** the writing lesson is a silent act of patriotism; M. Hamel takes a last look at forty years; Hauser spelling with the babies is the story's most moving image.

## "Vive La France!"

The church clock strikes twelve, then the **Angelus**; at the same moment the trumpets of the Prussians returning from drill sound under the windows. M. Hamel stands up, very pale and taller than Franz has ever seen him. He tries to speak — *my friends…* — but something chokes him. He turns to the blackboard, takes a piece of chalk, and bearing on it with all his might writes as large as he can: **"Vive La France!"** Then he leans his head against the wall and, without a word, makes a gesture with his hand: school is dismissed, you may go.

The story ends in silence. The teacher who could not finish his sentence finishes it on the blackboard; the two words are his last lesson and his farewell.

**In one breath:** at noon the Prussian trumpets sound; M. Hamel, unable to speak, writes "Vive La France!" and dismisses the class with a gesture.

## Themes and the writer's message

- **Language and identity.** A people's language is their link to their past, culture and freedom; the order from Berlin attacks exactly that. Hence "the key to their prison".
- **Linguistic chauvinism.** Imposing one's language on a conquered people — making even the pigeons "sing in German" — is a form of tyranny. Daudet shows it from the victims' side.
- **Procrastination and regret.** Franz, the parents and M. Hamel all "had time enough"; the story shows how losing something makes us value it. The change in Franz's attitude to books and to M. Hamel in a single morning is the story's emotional centre.
- **Patriotism in small acts.** The villagers on the back benches, the Sunday clothes, the new copies with "France, Alsace", the final two words on the board — no speeches, just quiet dignity.
- **The teacher's role.** M. Hamel is strict, fair, self-critical and devoted; the story is a tribute to teachers whose worth we see only when they leave.

**In one breath:** the story says: your language is your freedom, do not wait for tomorrow to value what you have, and real patriotism is quiet.

## Characters in two lines each

- **Franz:** a lazy, truant-loving schoolboy who, on the last day, discovers love for his language, his books and his teacher. The reader sees the story through his change.
- **M. Hamel:** French teacher for forty years; strict (the ruler) yet gentle on the last day; honest enough to blame himself; dignified in grief. Symbol of the teacher as guardian of language and culture.
- **Old Hauser and the villagers:** the former mayor, the former postmaster and others who come to honour the teacher and their lost country; Hauser spelling with the children shows regret and love for the language.
- **Wachter, the blacksmith:** his remark on the way to school is the first hint of the news.

**In one breath:** Franz changes, M. Hamel endures, the villagers honour — each embodies one thread of the theme.
