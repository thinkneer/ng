# Ch 6 · Evolution

Evolution is the story of how today's living things came from earlier forms over billions of years. This chapter moves from the origin of life to the evidence that evolution happened, to the mechanism Darwin proposed, the maths of Hardy and Weinberg, and finally our own ancestry. Learn the examples precisely: homologous vs analogous organs, Darwin's finches, peppered moths and the brain sizes of human ancestors are favourites.

## Origin of life

The universe is very old, almost 20 billion years by NCERT's account, and Earth formed about **4.5 billion years** ago. Early Earth had no atmosphere as we know it; water vapour, methane, carbon dioxide and ammonia released from molten material covered the surface. Ultraviolet rays from the Sun split water into hydrogen and oxygen, and oxygen combined with ammonia and methane to form water, carbon dioxide and other gases. Life appeared about **4 billion years** ago.

Several ideas were proposed. Early Greek thinkers believed units of life called spores travelled to different planets (**panspermia**). For a long time people believed in **spontaneous generation**: life arising from decaying matter. **Louis Pasteur** disproved it: in pre-sterilised flasks no life came from killed yeast, while in flasks open to air new organisms appeared.

**Oparin** (Russia) and **Haldane** (England) proposed that the first life came from pre-existing non-living organic molecules such as RNA and protein, and that its formation was preceded by **chemical evolution**: the formation of diverse organic molecules from inorganic ones under the conditions of early Earth (high temperature, volcanic storms, a reducing atmosphere of CH₄, NH₃ and water vapour).

In **1953**, **S. L. Miller** tested this. In a closed flask he passed electric discharges through **CH₄, H₂, NH₃ and water vapour at 800 °C**, and **amino acids** formed. Others obtained sugars, nitrogen bases, pigments and fats in similar experiments. The first non-cellular forms of life could have appeared about 3 billion years ago as giant molecules (RNA, proteins, polysaccharides) that could reproduce, and the first cellular forms about 2000 million years ago.

**In one breath:** Earth about 4.5 billion years old, life about 4 billion. Pasteur disproved spontaneous generation; Oparin and Haldane proposed chemical evolution; Miller (1953) made amino acids from CH₄, H₂, NH₃ and water vapour with electric discharge at 800 °C.

## Evidences for evolution

**Fossils.** Fossils are remains of hard parts of life forms preserved in rocks. Rock layers are laid down one above another, so each layer shows life of its time. Older layers have different, often simpler, life forms than younger ones, and many are extinct (dinosaurs, for example). The age of fossils is found by **radioactive dating**. Fossils show that life forms have changed over time.

**Comparative anatomy and morphology.** Similarities and differences between organisms today and those of the past reveal common ancestry.

- **Homologous organs** have the **same basic structure** (and origin) but perform **different functions**. The forelimbs of whales, bats, cheetahs and humans all have humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals and phalanges, but they swim, fly, run and grasp. Vertebrate hearts and brains are homologous too. In plants, the **thorns of *Bougainvillea*** and the **tendrils of *Cucurbita*** are homologous. Homology arises from **divergent evolution**: one ancestral structure adapted to different needs.
- **Analogous organs** have **different structures** but perform **similar functions**. The wings of a butterfly and of a bird; the eye of an octopus and of a mammal; the flippers of a penguin and of a dolphin; and the sweet potato (a root modification) and the potato (a stem modification) are analogous. Analogy arises from **convergent evolution**: different structures evolving for the same function in similar habitats.

| Basis | Homologous organs | Analogous organs |
|---|---|---|
| Structure and origin | same | different |
| Function | different | similar |
| Evolution | divergent | convergent |
| Examples | forelimbs of whale, bat, cheetah, human; thorn of *Bougainvillea* and tendril of *Cucurbita* | wings of butterfly and bird; eyes of octopus and mammal; potato and sweet potato |

**Molecular similarities.** Similar proteins and genes performing a given function in different organisms point to common ancestry.

**Embryology.** Ernst Haeckel noted features common to all vertebrate embryos that are absent in adults, such as a row of vestigial gill slits behind the head, which are functional only in fish. **Karl Ernst von Baer** disproved the idea that embryos pass through the adult stages of other animals.

**Natural selection in action.** In England, before industrialisation set in (around 1850), collections showed more **white-winged** peppered moths on trees than dark ones; collections made after industrialisation (around 1920) showed more **dark-winged** moths. After industrialisation, soot darkened the tree trunks and killed the light-coloured **lichens** covering them, so white moths were easily spotted by predators while dark ones were camouflaged. In unpolluted areas white moths remained common. The selection worked on existing variation; no moth changed its colour. Lichens are also indicators of air pollution, since they cannot grow in polluted areas. Similar human-driven selection is seen in the resistance of weeds to herbicides, pests to pesticides and microbes to antibiotics, over months or years, not centuries: this is **anthropogenic evolution**.

:::tip Thorn and tendril
The thorn of *Bougainvillea* and the tendril of *Cucurbita* are homologous (both modified stems); potato and sweet potato are analogous (stem vs root).
:::

**In one breath:** Fossils in rock layers (dated radioactively); homologous organs (same structure, different function, divergent evolution) and analogous organs (different structure, same function, convergent evolution); molecular similarity; embryology (von Baer corrected Haeckel); peppered moths and drug resistance show natural selection at work.

## Adaptive radiation

**Adaptive radiation** is the evolution of different species in a geographical area, starting from one point and spreading to other areas (habitats). Charles **Darwin** saw a striking example on the **Galapagos Islands**: a variety of small black birds, now called **Darwin's finches**. From an original seed-eating ancestor, finches evolved with beaks altered for different foods, including insects, and became different species.

Another example is the **Australian marsupials**. From an ancestral stock, many marsupials evolved in Australia, each different from the others: the koala, kangaroo, wombat, sugar glider, Tasmanian wolf, marsupial mole and others.

When more than one adaptive radiation occurs in an isolated area, representing different habitats, the result is **convergent evolution**. **Placental mammals** in other parts of the world show adaptive radiation into forms that look like the matching marsupials: placental wolf and Tasmanian wolf, flying squirrel and flying phalanger, mole and marsupial mole, lemur and spotted cuscus, anteater and numbat.

**In one breath:** Adaptive radiation = many species from one ancestor spreading into different habitats: Darwin's finches (Galapagos) and Australian marsupials. Look-alike placental and marsupial pairs (wolf and Tasmanian wolf) show convergent evolution.

## Darwin's theory and the mechanism of evolution

During his voyage on **HMS Beagle**, Darwin saw that existing living forms share similarities with each other and with forms that lived millions of years ago, many now extinct. He concluded that populations vary, and that variations which help survival and reproduction become more common. Alfred **Wallace**, working in the **Malay Archipelago**, reached similar conclusions.

Darwin's idea rests on two key concepts: **branching descent** and **natural selection**. Members of a population vary, resources are limited, and those better fitted to their environment leave more progeny. For Darwin, **fitness** means **reproductive fitness**: the ability to leave more offspring. Over generations, nature selects the fit, and new types of organisms arise.

What causes variation? Darwin did not know. Working on the evening primrose (***Oenothera lamarckiana***), **Hugo de Vries** proposed that evolution is driven by **mutation**: large, sudden changes. He called single-step large mutation **saltation**. Darwinian variations are small and directional, while de Vries' mutations are random and directionless. Today we know that mutation and genetic recombination supply variation and natural selection sorts it.

**In one breath:** Darwin (HMS Beagle) and Wallace (Malay Archipelago): branching descent + natural selection; fitness = reproductive fitness. de Vries (*Oenothera*): mutations, saltation; random and directionless, unlike Darwin's small directional variations.

## Hardy–Weinberg principle

In a population, the frequency of each allele can be measured. The **Hardy–Weinberg principle** says that allele frequencies in a population are **stable and constant from generation to generation** if nothing disturbs them. This state is **genetic equilibrium**. The total of all alleles at a locus is constant, so the frequencies add up to 1.

:::formula Hardy–Weinberg equation
$p + q = 1$ and $p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1$, where $p$, $q$ are allele frequencies; $p^2$, $q^2$ are homozygote frequencies; $2pq$ is the heterozygote frequency.
:::

When measured frequencies differ from the expected values, evolution is happening. **Five factors** disturb the equilibrium: **gene migration or gene flow** (alleles added or lost as individuals move between populations), **genetic drift** (chance changes in small populations; if a small group founds a new population, its allele frequencies may differ sharply from the original, the **founder effect**), **mutation**, **genetic recombination** during gamete formation, and **natural selection**.

:::skeleton Worked example: carrier frequency
*In a population, 1 in 400 people has a recessive disorder (aa). Find the frequency of carriers.*<br>
$q^2 = 1/400 = 0.0025$, so $q = 0.05$ [1]<br>
$p = 1 - q = 0.95$ [½]<br>
Carriers $= 2pq = 2 \times 0.95 \times 0.05 = 0.095$, about **9.5%** [1½]
:::

**In one breath:** Hardy–Weinberg: allele frequencies stay constant in an undisturbed population, $p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1$. Gene flow, genetic drift (founder effect), mutation, recombination and natural selection disturb it.

## Types of natural selection

Natural selection can act on a range of variation in three ways. Imagine a trait such as body size measured across a population and plotted as a bell curve.

- **Stabilising selection**: individuals with the **mean** (average) value are favoured; more individuals acquire the mean character value and the curve becomes narrower and taller.
- **Directional selection**: individuals at **one extreme** are favoured; more individuals acquire a value other than the mean, and the peak shifts in one direction.
- **Disruptive selection**: individuals at **both extremes** are favoured; the curve develops two peaks.

## A brief account of evolution and the origin of humans

About 2000 million years ago the first cellular life forms appeared. By about 500 million years ago invertebrates were active, and jawless fish probably evolved around 350 million years ago. The first organisms to invade land were plants. Fish with stout, strong fins (lobefins) could move on land and go back to water; a lobefin, the **coelacanth**, was caught in South Africa in 1938, though it was thought extinct. These animals gave rise to the first **amphibians**, and amphibians to **reptiles**, which laid thick-shelled eggs that do not dry up. About 200 million years ago reptiles of various sizes dominated; dinosaurs disappeared suddenly about **65 million years ago**, for reasons still debated. Mammals, which were small at first, then spread; the pouched mammals of Australia survived because of the lack of competition from other mammals.

**Human evolution.** About **15 million years ago**, primates called ***Dryopithecus*** and ***Ramapithecus*** existed. They were hairy and walked like gorillas and chimpanzees; *Ramapithecus* was more man-like, *Dryopithecus* more ape-like. Man-like bones have been found in Ethiopia and Tanzania. About **2 million years ago**, ***Australopithecines*** lived in the East African grasslands; they probably hunted with stone weapons but essentially ate fruit.

| Ancestor | When | Brain capacity | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Homo habilis* | about 2 million years ago | **650–800 cc** | first human-like being; probably did not eat meat |
| *Homo erectus* | about 1.5 million years ago | about **900 cc** | probably ate meat |
| Neanderthal man | 1,00,000–40,000 years ago | **1400 cc** | near east and central Asia; buried their dead |
| *Homo sapiens* | arose in Africa | — | spread across continents; became distinct races |

During the ice age, between 75,000 and 10,000 years ago, modern *Homo sapiens* arose. Pre-historic cave art developed about 18,000 years ago (one example is the Bhimbetka rock shelter in Raisen district, Madhya Pradesh). Agriculture began about 10,000 years ago, and human settlements started.

:::tip Brain sizes
Habilis 650–800 cc · erectus 900 cc · Neanderthal 1400 cc. A favourite MCQ: arrange them or match them.
:::

**In one breath:** Life moved from water to land through lobefins (coelacanth) and amphibians; dinosaurs vanished 65 million years ago. Human line: *Dryopithecus*, *Ramapithecus* (15 mya) → *Australopithecus* (2 mya) → *H. habilis* (650–800 cc) → *H. erectus* (900 cc) → Neanderthal (1400 cc) → *H. sapiens* from Africa.
