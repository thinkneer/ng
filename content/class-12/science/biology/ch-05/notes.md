# Ch 5 · Molecular Basis of Inheritance

Chapter 4 talked about "genes" as abstract factors. This chapter opens the box: what a gene is made of, how it copies itself, how it is read into RNA and protein, how it is switched on and off, and how reading whole genomes lets us identify a person from a drop of blood. It is the longest chapter of the unit and the richest source of 3- and 5-mark questions.

## The structure of DNA

DNA is a long polymer of **deoxyribonucleotides**. Each nucleotide has three parts: a **nitrogenous base**, a **pentose sugar** (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA) and a **phosphate group**. The bases are of two kinds: **purines** (adenine, guanine) and **pyrimidines** (cytosine and thymine in DNA; cytosine and uracil in RNA). Thymine is 5-methyl uracil.

A base joins the 1′ carbon of the sugar by an **N-glycosidic linkage** to form a **nucleoside**; adding a phosphate to the 5′ carbon by a phosphoester bond makes a **nucleotide**. Nucleotides join through **3′–5′ phosphodiester bonds** into a chain with a free phosphate at the **5′ end** and a free 3′-OH at the **3′ end**. In RNA, every ribose also carries a 2′-OH group.

DNA was first isolated as an acidic substance called "nuclein" by Friedrich Meischer in 1869. In 1953 James **Watson** and Francis **Crick**, using X-ray diffraction data from Maurice Wilkins and Rosalind Franklin, proposed the **double helix**. Erwin **Chargaff**'s rule gave a key clue: in double-stranded DNA, **A = T and G = C**.

Main features of the double helix:

- Two polynucleotide chains, with a sugar–phosphate backbone outside and bases projecting inside.
- The chains are **antiparallel**: one runs 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′.
- Bases pair by hydrogen bonds: **A with T by two bonds**, **G with C by three bonds**. A purine always faces a pyrimidine, so the distance between the strands stays uniform.
- The chains coil in a **right-handed** helix. The **pitch is 3.4 nm** with about **10 base pairs per turn**, so adjacent base pairs are **0.34 nm** apart.
- Base pairs stack on one another, which adds to the stability of the helix.

Crick also proposed the **central dogma**: genetic information flows **DNA → RNA → protein**. In some viruses it runs in reverse, RNA → DNA (reverse transcription).

## Packaging DNA

The length of DNA is the number of base pairs × 0.34 nm. A human diploid cell has 6.6 × 10⁹ bp, so its DNA is about **2.2 m** long, yet the nucleus is only about 10⁻⁶ m across. *E. coli* has 4.6 × 10⁶ bp, about 1.36 mm of DNA. So DNA must be packed very tightly.

In prokaryotes, which have no nucleus, the negatively charged DNA is held in large loops by positively charged proteins in a region called the **nucleoid**.

In eukaryotes, DNA wraps around **histones**, proteins rich in the basic amino acids **lysine and arginine**, which makes them positively charged. Eight histone molecules form a **histone octamer**. The negatively charged DNA winds around it to form a **nucleosome**; a typical nucleosome holds about **200 bp** of DNA. Under the electron microscope, chromatin looks like **"beads on a string"**. Nucleosomes coil further into chromatin fibres, which fold into the chromosomes seen at metaphase; this higher packing needs **non-histone chromosomal (NHC) proteins**.

Chromatin that is loosely packed and stains light is **euchromatin**; it is transcriptionally active. Densely packed, dark-staining chromatin is **heterochromatin**, which is inactive.

## The search for the genetic material

**Transforming principle (Griffith, 1928).** *Streptococcus pneumoniae* has two strains: S (smooth, capsule, kills mice) and R (rough, no capsule, harmless). Live S kills mice; live R does not; heat-killed S does not. But a mixture of **heat-killed S and live R killed mice**, and live S bacteria were recovered from them. Something from the dead S cells had **transformed** the R cells. Griffith could not say what.

**Biochemical proof (Avery, MacLeod and McCarty, 1933–44).** They purified DNA, RNA and protein from heat-killed S cells. Protein-digesting enzymes (proteases) and RNase did not stop transformation, but **DNase did**. So DNA was the transforming principle, though not everyone was convinced.

**Unequivocal proof (Hershey and Chase, 1952).** They grew bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria, on media with radioactive **phosphorus (³²P)**, which labels DNA, or radioactive **sulfur (³⁵S)**, which labels protein. After infection, blending and centrifugation, the bacteria infected with ³²P-labelled phages were radioactive, while those infected with ³⁵S phages were not. Only DNA entered the bacteria, so **DNA is the genetic material**.

What makes a good genetic material? It must be able to **replicate**, be **chemically and structurally stable**, allow **slow changes (mutation)** for evolution, and **express itself** as Mendelian characters. DNA is the better choice for most organisms. RNA's 2′-OH makes it reactive and less stable, and it mutates faster. DNA's thymine (instead of uracil) adds stability. RNA, however, can act as a catalyst and was probably the **first genetic material** (the RNA world); DNA evolved later as a more stable store. For expressing traits, RNA is better: it is directly used to make proteins.

## DNA replication

Watson and Crick suggested that each strand serves as a template for a new strand, so each daughter molecule has **one old and one new strand**: **semiconservative replication**.

**Meselson and Stahl (1958)** proved it. They grew *E. coli* for many generations in medium with ¹⁵NH₄Cl, so all DNA was "heavy" (¹⁵N). They moved the cells to ordinary ¹⁴N medium and separated DNA by density in a **caesium chloride (CsCl) density gradient**. After one generation (about 20 minutes), all DNA was of **hybrid** (intermediate) density. After two generations (40 minutes), DNA was half hybrid and half light. This is exactly what semiconservative replication predicts. Taylor and colleagues showed the same in *Vicia faba* (faba bean) using radioactive thymidine.

**The machinery.** The main enzyme is **DNA-dependent DNA polymerase**. It is fast and accurate: *E. coli*, with 4.6 × 10⁶ bp, finishes replication in about **18 minutes**. Deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates act both as substrates and as the energy source. The two strands cannot separate along their whole length at once, so replication happens in a small opening called the **replication fork**.

DNA polymerase can add nucleotides **only in the 5′→3′ direction**. So on the template strand running 3′→5′, the new strand is made **continuously** (leading strand). On the other template (5′→3′), it is made **discontinuously** in short fragments (lagging strand), which are joined by **DNA ligase**. Replication starts at a definite region, the **origin of replication (ori)**. In eukaryotes it happens in the **S phase** of the cell cycle.

## Transcription

**Transcription** copies the information of one DNA strand into RNA. Only one strand is copied, for two reasons: if both were copied, the two RNAs would be complementary and pair up into double-stranded RNA, which would not be translated, and the same segment would code for two different proteins.

A **transcription unit** has three regions: a **promoter**, the **structural gene** and a **terminator**. The strand with polarity 3′→5′ acts as the **template strand**. The other strand, 5′→3′, has the same sequence as the RNA (with T instead of U) and is called the **coding strand**, although it codes for nothing itself. The promoter lies towards the 5′ end (upstream) of the structural gene, as defined on the coding strand, and is where RNA polymerase binds; the terminator lies at the 3′ end (downstream).

A gene can be defined as the functional unit of inheritance; a **cistron** is a DNA segment coding for one polypeptide. Eukaryotic structural genes are **monocistronic**; bacterial ones are mostly **polycistronic**. Eukaryotic genes are **split genes**: coding sequences, **exons**, are interrupted by non-coding **introns**, which do not appear in the mature RNA.

**In bacteria**, there are three major RNAs: **mRNA** (template), **tRNA** (brings amino acids, reads the code) and **rRNA** (structural and catalytic role in ribosomes). A single DNA-dependent RNA polymerase makes all of them. It binds the promoter with the help of the **σ (sigma) factor** to start, and the **ρ (rho) factor** helps it stop. In bacteria, mRNA needs no processing and translation can start even before transcription ends, since there is no nuclear membrane.

**In eukaryotes**, there are three RNA polymerases in the nucleus: **RNA polymerase I** makes the 28S, 18S and 5.8S rRNAs; **RNA polymerase II** makes the precursor of mRNA, the **heterogeneous nuclear RNA (hnRNA)**; **RNA polymerase III** makes tRNA, 5S rRNA and snRNAs. The hnRNA is processed: **splicing** removes introns and joins exons; **capping** adds an unusual nucleotide, methyl guanosine triphosphate, at the 5′ end; **tailing** adds 200–300 adenylate residues (poly-A tail) at the 3′ end. The processed hnRNA, now mRNA, goes to the cytoplasm.

## The genetic code

How do four bases code for twenty amino acids? George **Gamow** argued that the code must be a **triplet**: 4³ = 64 combinations is more than enough for 20 amino acids. Har Gobind **Khorana** made RNA molecules with defined base sequences chemically; Marshall **Nirenberg**'s cell-free system for protein synthesis decoded them; **Severo Ochoa**'s enzyme, polynucleotide phosphorylase, helped make RNA of defined sequence without a template.

Features of the code:

- It is a **triplet**: 64 codons, of which **61 code for amino acids** and **3 are stop codons** (UAA, UAG, UGA) that code for none.
- **Unambiguous and specific**: one codon codes for only one amino acid.
- **Degenerate**: some amino acids are coded by more than one codon.
- Read in mRNA **continuously**, with no punctuation between codons.
- **Nearly universal**: UUU codes for phenylalanine from bacteria to humans (a few exceptions in mitochondria and some protozoans).
- **AUG** has a dual function: it codes for **methionine** and acts as the **start codon**.

Mutations show the code's nature. Inserting or deleting one or two bases shifts the reading frame from that point (a **frameshift**), garbling everything after it; inserting or deleting three bases adds or removes one amino acid and keeps the rest intact. This supports the idea that the code is read as triplets without gaps.

**tRNA, the adapter.** Crick predicted an adapter molecule to read the code and carry amino acids. tRNA has an **anticodon loop** with bases complementary to the codon, and an **amino-acid acceptor end** where its amino acid attaches. Each tRNA is specific for one amino acid; a special initiator tRNA carries the first amino acid; there are no tRNAs for stop codons. tRNA looks like a **clover leaf** in two dimensions and an **inverted L** in three.

## Translation

**Translation** joins amino acids into a polypeptide in the order set by mRNA codons; the bond formed is a peptide bond.

1. **Charging (aminoacylation) of tRNA**: each amino acid is activated with **ATP** and attached to its tRNA.
2. **Initiation**: the ribosome has two subunits. When the small subunit meets the mRNA, translation begins at the start codon **AUG**, recognised by the initiator tRNA carrying methionine.
3. **Elongation**: charged tRNAs arrive one by one; the ribosome has two sites for neighbouring tRNAs so peptide bonds can form between their amino acids. The ribosome moves codon by codon along the mRNA. Peptide bond formation is catalysed by rRNA (in bacteria, **23S rRNA** acts as a **ribozyme**).
4. **Termination**: at a stop codon, a **release factor** binds and the finished polypeptide is released.

mRNA also carries **untranslated regions (UTRs)** before the start codon (5′ UTR) and after the stop codon (3′ UTR); they are needed for efficient translation.

```ng-steps

title: Translation in four moves

step: **Initiation**: the small ribosomal subunit binds mRNA; the initiator tRNA (methionine) pairs with **AUG**.
step: **Elongation**: charged tRNAs arrive codon by codon; **23S rRNA (ribozyme)** forms peptide bonds; the ribosome moves along.
```

**In one breath:** tRNA charging (ATP) → initiation at AUG with initiator tRNA → elongation with peptide bonds made by rRNA (23S ribozyme) → release factor at the stop codon. UTRs flank the coding region.

## Regulation of gene expression: the lac operon

Cells do not make every protein all the time. In eukaryotes, expression can be regulated at transcription, at processing (splicing), at mRNA transport from nucleus to cytoplasm, and at translation. In bacteria, control is mostly at the start of transcription, often through an **operon**: a set of genes with related functions controlled together.

The **lac operon** of *E. coli*, explained by François **Jacob** and Jacques **Monod**, controls the use of lactose. It has a regulatory gene **i** and three structural genes:

- **z** codes for **β-galactosidase**, which splits lactose into galactose and glucose;
- **y** codes for **permease**, which increases the cell's permeability to lactose;
- **a** codes for **transacetylase**.

The **i gene** codes for a **repressor** protein. It is made all the time (constitutively). In the **absence of lactose**, the repressor binds the **operator** and blocks RNA polymerase, so z, y and a are not transcribed. When **lactose** (the **inducer**) is present, it enters the cell through the small amount of permease always present, binds the repressor and **inactivates** it. The repressor can no longer bind the operator, RNA polymerase transcribes the structural genes, and the enzymes for lactose use are made. Because the repressor switches the operon off, this is **negative regulation**.

## Human Genome Project and DNA fingerprinting

The **Human Genome Project (HGP)**, launched in **1990** and completed in **2003**, was called a mega project because of its size and cost. Its goals were to identify all the human genes, determine the sequence of the roughly **3 billion** base pairs, store the information in databases, improve tools for analysis, transfer the technology to industry, and address the ethical, legal and social issues raised.

Two approaches were used: identifying all genes expressed as RNA (**expressed sequence tags, ESTs**), and sequencing the whole genome, coding and non-coding, then assigning functions (**sequence annotation**). DNA was broken into fragments, cloned in hosts using vectors such as **BAC** (bacterial artificial chromosome) and **YAC** (yeast artificial chromosome), and sequenced with automated sequencers based on Frederick Sanger's method.

Salient findings:

- The genome has **3164.7 million** base pairs.
- An average gene has about 3000 bases; the largest, **dystrophin**, has 2.4 million.
- There are about **30,000** genes, far fewer than expected; the functions of over half are unknown.
- **99.9%** of the bases are the same in all people.
- **Less than 2%** of the genome codes for proteins; a large part is **repeated sequences**.
- **Chromosome 1** has the most genes (2968); the **Y** chromosome has the fewest (231).
- About **1.4 million** single-base differences (**SNPs**, "snips") have been located.

Whole genomes of other organisms, including the rice genome, have also been sequenced, helping crop improvement.

**DNA fingerprinting**, developed by Alec **Jeffreys**, compares the repetitive DNA of individuals. A small part of the genome is made of short sequences repeated many times. Spun in a density gradient, bulk DNA forms a major peak and these repeats form small **satellite** peaks; this is **satellite DNA**. Satellites (micro- and mini-satellites) do not code for proteins and show high **polymorphism**: they differ from person to person and are inherited. Jeffreys used a **variable number tandem repeat (VNTR)** as a probe. Sizes of VNTRs vary from 0.1 to 20 kb.

Steps: isolate DNA → digest it with **restriction endonucleases** → separate the fragments by **gel electrophoresis** → transfer (blot) them to a nitrocellulose or nylon membrane (**Southern blotting**) → **hybridise** with a labelled VNTR probe → detect the hybridised fragments by **autoradiography**. The band pattern is unique to each person except identical twins. Uses: forensic science, paternity disputes, and studies of population and genetic diversity.
