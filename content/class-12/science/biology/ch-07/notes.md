# Ch 7 · Human Health and Disease

Health is not just the absence of disease. This chapter covers what keeps us healthy and what breaks health down: common infectious diseases and their causes, how the immune system fights back, and three big modern problems, AIDS, cancer and drug abuse. Board questions love pathogen names, the malaria life cycle, antibody structure, types of immunity and the effects of drugs, so learn the names exactly.

## Health and common diseases

Health means a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being. It is affected by genetic disorders, infections and lifestyle (food, water, rest, exercise, habits). Diseases that pass from one person to another are **infectious**; those that do not, like cancer, are **non-infectious**. Disease-causing organisms are **pathogens**: bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoans, helminths and so on.

| Disease | Pathogen (type) | Spread | Key points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typhoid | *Salmonella typhi* (bacterium) | contaminated food and water | sustained high fever (39–40 °C), weakness, stomach pain, constipation, headache, loss of appetite; intestinal perforation in severe cases; confirmed by **Widal test**; "Typhoid Mary" (Mary Mallon), a cook, was a carrier |
| Pneumonia | *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae* (bacteria) | droplets or aerosols from an infected person; sharing glasses and utensils | alveoli fill with fluid, breathing problems; fever, chills, cough, headache; lips and fingernails may turn grey to bluish |
| Common cold | **rhinoviruses** | droplets, contaminated objects | infects nose and respiratory passage but **not the lungs**; nasal congestion, sore throat, cough; lasts 3–7 days |

| Amoebiasis (amoebic dysentery) | *Entamoeba histolytica* (protozoan) | houseflies carry cysts to food and water | lives in large intestine; constipation, abdominal pain, cramps, stools with excess mucus and blood clots |

Prevention depends on **personal hygiene** (clean body, safe drinking water and food), **public hygiene** (proper waste disposal, cleaning of water reservoirs, standard practices in public catering), avoiding close contact with infected people for air-borne diseases, and **controlling vectors**. For mosquito-borne diseases such as malaria, filariasis, **dengue** and **chikungunya** (the last two spread by *Aedes* mosquitoes), we avoid stagnant water near homes, clean coolers regularly, use mosquito nets and introduce fish like ***Gambusia*** into ponds to eat mosquito larvae. Vaccines and antibiotics have controlled smallpox (now eradicated), polio, diphtheria, pneumonia and tetanus.

## The malaria parasite's life cycle

*Plasmodium* needs two hosts: humans and the female *Anopheles* mosquito.

1. An infected female *Anopheles* bites a person and injects **sporozoites** (the infectious form) with its saliva.
2. The parasites multiply asexually first in **liver cells**, then attack **red blood cells**, multiply there and burst them.
3. The bursting RBCs release a toxic substance, **haemozoin**, which causes the **chill and high fever recurring every three to four days**.
4. Some parasites in the blood develop into **gametocytes** (sexual stages).
5. When a female *Anopheles* bites the infected person, it takes up gametocytes. **Fertilisation** and development take place in the mosquito's **stomach** (gut).
6. Sporozoites form and move to the mosquito's **salivary glands**, ready to infect the next person.

So asexual reproduction happens in the human (liver and RBCs) and sexual reproduction in the mosquito.

## Immunity

The body's ability to fight disease-causing organisms is **immunity**. It is of two kinds.

**Innate immunity** is non-specific and present from birth. It works through four kinds of barriers:

- **Physical barriers**: skin, and the mucus coating of the respiratory, gastrointestinal and urogenital tracts, which traps microbes.
- **Physiological barriers**: acid in the stomach, saliva in the mouth and tears from the eyes.
- **Cellular barriers**: leucocytes such as polymorpho-nuclear leucocytes (PMNL, neutrophils) and monocytes, natural killer lymphocytes in the blood, and macrophages in tissues, which phagocytose and destroy microbes.
- **Cytokine barriers**: virus-infected cells secrete **interferons**, which protect non-infected cells from further viral infection.

**Acquired immunity** is pathogen-specific and has **memory**. On the first encounter with a pathogen, the body makes a low-intensity **primary response**. A later encounter with the same pathogen triggers a much stronger **secondary (anamnestic) response**, because the body remembers the first one.

Two kinds of lymphocytes carry out acquired immunity. **B-lymphocytes** make proteins called **antibodies** into the blood; this is **humoral immunity**. **T-lymphocytes** do not secrete antibodies but help B cells make them, and directly mediate **cell-mediated immunity (CMI)**. CMI is responsible for **graft rejection**: the body can tell "self" from "non-self", which is why tissue and blood group matching is needed before transplants, and why patients take immunosuppressants afterwards.

Each **antibody** molecule has **four peptide chains**: two small **light chains** and two longer **heavy chains**, written **H₂L₂**. They form a Y shape, with antigen-binding sites at the tips of the arms. Different types of antibodies are **IgA, IgM, IgE and IgG**.

**Active and passive immunity.** When the body makes its own antibodies after exposure to antigens, by infection or by deliberate injection of dead or weakened microbes, it is **active immunity**: slow to start but long-lasting. When ready-made antibodies are given to the body, it is **passive immunity**. Examples: **colostrum**, the yellowish first milk, is rich in **IgA** antibodies that protect the infant; the foetus receives some antibodies from the mother through the placenta; for quick protection, as against tetanus or snakebite (antivenom), pre-formed antibodies or antitoxins are injected.

**Vaccination and immunisation.** A vaccine is a preparation of antigenic proteins of a pathogen or inactivated or weakened pathogens. It triggers a primary response and leaves **memory B and T cells**, so a real infection later meets a fast, strong secondary response. Recombinant DNA technology lets us make antigenic polypeptides in bacteria or yeast; the **hepatitis B vaccine** is produced in **yeast** this way.

**Allergies** are exaggerated responses of the immune system to certain antigens in the environment, called **allergens** (dust mites, pollen, animal dander). The antibodies involved are **IgE**. Allergy is caused by the release of chemicals such as **histamine and serotonin** from **mast cells**; symptoms include sneezing, watery eyes, a running nose and difficulty in breathing. Drugs like **anti-histamines, adrenalin and steroids** reduce the symptoms. Modern lifestyles, with less exposure to microbes in early life, may make people more allergy-prone.

**Autoimmunity** happens when, because of genetic or unknown reasons, the body attacks its own cells. **Rheumatoid arthritis** is an example.

**The immune system** has lymphoid organs, tissues, cells and antibodies. **Primary lymphoid organs**, the **bone marrow** and **thymus**, are where immature lymphocytes form and mature into antigen-sensitive cells. The bone marrow makes all blood cells, including lymphocytes. The thymus is large at birth and shrinks with age. **Secondary lymphoid organs**, where lymphocytes meet antigens and multiply, include the **spleen** (a filter of blood, with a reservoir of erythrocytes), **lymph nodes** (trap antigens from lymph and tissue fluid), **tonsils**, **Peyer's patches** of the small intestine and the **appendix**. Lymphoid tissue in the linings of the respiratory, digestive and urogenital tracts is **MALT** (mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue), about **50%** of the lymphoid tissue in the body.

:::tip Active vs passive
Active: the body makes its own antibodies (infection, vaccine), slow and long-lasting. Passive: ready-made antibodies are given (colostrum, antitoxin), quick but short-lived.
:::

**In one breath:** Innate immunity uses physical, physiological, cellular and cytokine (interferon) barriers; acquired immunity has memory (primary vs secondary response), with B cells (antibodies, H₂L₂; IgA, IgM, IgE, IgG) and T cells (CMI, graft rejection). Vaccines leave memory cells; allergy involves IgE, mast cells and histamine; bone marrow and thymus are primary lymphoid organs.

## AIDS

**AIDS** (Acquired Immuno Deficiency Syndrome) is a deficiency of the immune system that is acquired during life, not inborn; "syndrome" means a group of symptoms. It was first reported in **1981**. It is caused by the **Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)**, a **retrovirus** with an **RNA genome** inside an envelope.

HIV spreads by sexual contact with an infected person, transfusion of contaminated blood and blood products, sharing infected needles (as among intravenous drug users), and from an infected mother to her child through the placenta. People with multiple sexual partners, drug addicts who take drugs intravenously, people who need repeated blood transfusions and children born to HIV-infected mothers are at high risk. HIV/AIDS does **not** spread by mere touch or physical contact; it spreads only through body fluids. There is always a gap, from a few months to many years (5–10 years), between infection and the appearance of symptoms.

How HIV works: it enters **macrophages**. There its RNA is copied into viral DNA by the enzyme **reverse transcriptase**; the viral DNA joins the host cell's DNA and directs the cell to make virus particles. So the macrophage keeps producing virus and acts as an **HIV factory**. HIV also enters **helper T-lymphocytes (T_H)**, multiplies, and the new viruses attack other helper T cells. The number of helper T cells falls steadily. The person suffers repeated fever, diarrhoea and weight loss, and becomes so immunodeficient that infections by bacteria (especially *Mycobacterium*), viruses, fungi and even parasites like *Toxoplasma* overwhelm them.

HIV infection is diagnosed by **ELISA** (enzyme-linked immuno-sorbent assay). **Anti-retroviral drugs** prolong life only partially; they cannot prevent death. Prevention is the best option: education about AIDS (NACO, the National AIDS Control Organisation, and NGOs work on this), safe blood from blood banks, disposable needles and syringes, free distribution of condoms, control of drug abuse, safe sex and regular check-ups for people at risk.

:::tip HIV factory
Macrophages keep producing virus (the "HIV factory"); helper T cells are the ones whose numbers fall.
:::

## Cancer

Normal cells stop dividing when they touch neighbouring cells; this is **contact inhibition**. Cancer cells lose this property, divide uncontrollably and form masses called **tumours**. **Benign tumours** stay confined to their original location and cause little damage. **Malignant tumours** are masses of proliferating **neoplastic** (tumour) cells that grow very fast, invade surrounding tissues, starve normal cells of nutrients and spread to distant sites through the blood. This spreading, the most feared property of malignant tumours, is **metastasis**.

**Causes.** Agents that transform normal cells into cancer cells are **carcinogens**. They include ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays), non-ionising radiation (UV), chemical carcinogens (for example, those in **tobacco smoke**, a major cause of lung cancer) and cancer-causing viruses, **oncogenic viruses**, which carry **viral oncogenes**. Normal cells also have genes called **cellular oncogenes (c-onc) or proto-oncogenes**; when activated under certain conditions, they can cause cancer.

**Detection.** Biopsy and **histopathological** study of a tissue; blood and bone marrow tests for increased cell counts (leukaemia); **radiography (X-rays)**, **computed tomography (CT)** and **magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)** for internal organs (CT uses X-rays to make a 3-D image; MRI uses strong magnetic fields and non-ionising radiation); antibodies against cancer-specific antigens; and molecular biology techniques to find cancer-related genes in people at risk.

**Treatment.** **Surgery**, **radiation therapy** (killing tumour cells while protecting normal tissue), **chemotherapy** (drugs that kill cancer cells, with side effects like hair loss and anaemia) and **immunotherapy**: tumour cells avoid detection by the immune system, so patients are given **biological response modifiers** such as **α-interferon**, which activate the immune system to destroy the tumour.

## Drugs and alcohol abuse

The drugs most commonly abused are opioids, cannabinoids and coca alkaloids, mostly obtained from flowering plants (some from fungi).

- **Opioids** bind to specific **opioid receptors** in the central nervous system and gastrointestinal tract. **Heroin** ("smack"), chemically **diacetylmorphine**, is a white, odourless, bitter crystalline compound obtained by acetylation of **morphine**, which is extracted from the latex of the poppy, ***Papaver somniferum***. It is taken by snorting or injection and is a **depressant** that slows body functions.
- **Cannabinoids** interact with cannabinoid receptors, mainly in the brain. They come from the inflorescences of ***Cannabis sativa***; its flower tops, leaves and resin give **marijuana, hashish, charas and ganja**. They are taken by inhalation or orally and **affect the cardiovascular system**.
- **Coca alkaloid** or **cocaine** comes from the coca plant ***Erythroxylum coca***. It interferes with the transport of the neurotransmitter **dopamine**. "Coke" or "crack" is usually snorted. It **stimulates the central nervous system**, producing euphoria and increased energy; excess causes hallucinations.
- Other plants with hallucinogenic properties include ***Atropa belladonna*** and ***Datura***. Drugs like **barbiturates, amphetamines, benzodiazepines** and **LSD**, which are used as medicines for depression and insomnia, are often abused.

**Tobacco** has been used for over 400 years. It contains **nicotine**, an alkaloid that stimulates the **adrenal gland** to release **adrenaline and noradrenaline**, raising blood pressure and heart rate. Smoking is linked to cancers of the lung, urinary bladder and throat, bronchitis, emphysema, coronary heart disease and gastric ulcer. Smoking raises **carbon monoxide** in the blood and lowers the concentration of haem-bound oxygen. Chewing tobacco is linked to oral cancer.

**Adolescence** (12–18 years) is a time of curiosity, need for adventure and excitement, and peer pressure, which often lead to first use. Repeated use leads to **addiction**: a psychological attachment to the feelings of euphoria. With repeated use, the body's tolerance rises and higher doses are needed. **Dependence** is the body's tendency to show unpleasant **withdrawal syndrome** (anxiety, shakiness, nausea, sweating) when regular use stops.

Effects include reckless behaviour, vandalism and violence; overdose can cause coma and death through respiratory failure, heart failure or brain haemorrhage. Warning signs: drop in academic performance, unexplained absence from school, lack of interest in personal hygiene, withdrawal, isolation, depression, fatigue, aggressive behaviour, changes in sleeping and eating habits. Intravenous drug use carries the risk of AIDS and hepatitis B. Chronic alcohol use damages the nervous system and liver (**cirrhosis**). Use during pregnancy harms the foetus. Some sportspersons misuse narcotic analgesics, anabolic steroids, diuretics and hormones; anabolic steroids cause, in females, masculinisation, increased aggressiveness, mood swings, abnormal menstrual cycles and deepening of voice; in males, acne, reduced size of testicles, decreased sperm production, kidney and liver problems, breast enlargement and premature baldness.

**Prevention**: avoid undue peer pressure; education and counselling to face problems and stress; seek help from parents and peers; look for danger signs; seek professional and medical help (psychologists, psychiatrists, de-addiction and rehabilitation programmes).
