# Ch 8 · Microbes in Human Welfare

Microbes are often thought of only as germs, but most are harmless and many are hugely useful. This short chapter is a tour of six places where microbes work for us: the kitchen, factories, sewage plants, biogas plants, farms (as pest-killers) and soil (as fertilisers). The marks come from matching each product or job to the right microbe, so keep the scientific names straight.

## Microbes in household products

**Curd.** *Lactobacillus* and other **lactic acid bacteria (LAB)** grow in milk and convert it to curd. They produce acids that coagulate and partially digest milk proteins. A small amount of curd added to fresh milk as an inoculum (starter) contains millions of LAB, which multiply at suitable temperatures. LAB also **increase vitamin B₁₂** in curd and check disease-causing microbes in our stomach.

**Dough and batter.** The dough for idli and dosa is fermented by bacteria; the puffed-up look comes from **carbon dioxide** gas. Dough for bread is fermented by **baker's yeast**, ***Saccharomyces cerevisiae***.

**Toddy**, a traditional drink of some parts of southern India, is made by fermenting sap from palms. Microbes are also used to ferment fish, soybean and bamboo shoots.

**Cheese.** Different microbes give cheeses their textures, flavours and tastes. The large holes in **Swiss cheese** come from a large amount of CO₂ produced by the bacterium ***Propionibacterium sharmanii***. **Roquefort cheese** is ripened by growing specific **fungi** on it.

**In one breath:** LAB (*Lactobacillus*) make curd and add vitamin B₁₂; yeast (*S. cerevisiae*) and bacteria raise dough with CO₂; toddy from palm sap; Swiss cheese holes from *Propionibacterium sharmanii*; Roquefort ripened by fungi.

## Microbes in industrial products

For industrial production, microbes are grown in very large vessels called **fermentors**.

**Fermented beverages.** ***Saccharomyces cerevisiae***, here called **brewer's yeast**, ferments malted cereals and fruit juices to produce ethanol. **Wine and beer** are made **without distillation**; **whisky, brandy and rum** are made by **distilling** the fermented broth.

**Antibiotics.** Antibiotics are chemicals produced by some microbes that kill or retard the growth of other (disease-causing) microbes. **Penicillin** was the first, discovered by chance: **Alexander Fleming**, working on *Staphylococci* bacteria, noticed that a mould growing on an unwashed culture plate stopped the bacteria growing around it. The mould was ***Penicillium notatum***. Its full potential as an effective antibiotic was established much later by **Ernest Chain and Howard Florey**; penicillin was used extensively to treat soldiers wounded in World War II. Fleming, Chain and Florey received the **Nobel Prize in 1945**. Antibiotics have greatly improved our ability to treat diseases like plague, whooping cough, diphtheria and leprosy.

## Microbes in sewage treatment

Cities produce large amounts of waste water, **sewage**, containing large amounts of organic matter and microbes, many of them pathogens. It cannot be dumped into rivers directly, so it is treated in **sewage treatment plants (STPs)** to make it less polluting. Treatment is mainly done by heterotrophic microbes naturally present in sewage, in two stages.

**Primary treatment** is physical. Floating debris is removed by sequential filtration, and grit (soil and small pebbles) by sedimentation. Solids that settle form the **primary sludge**; the liquid above, the **effluent**, goes on to secondary treatment.

**Secondary (biological) treatment.** The primary effluent passes into large **aeration tanks**, where it is constantly agitated mechanically and air is pumped in. Useful aerobic microbes grow vigorously as **flocs**: masses of bacteria associated with fungal filaments in a mesh-like structure. They consume most of the organic matter, which sharply reduces the **BOD** (biochemical oxygen demand) of the effluent.

**BOD** is the amount of oxygen that would be consumed if all the organic matter in one litre of water were oxidised by bacteria. Sewage is treated until its BOD is reduced; a high BOD means the water is highly polluted with organic matter.

Once BOD is low enough, the effluent goes to a **settling tank**, where the flocs settle as **activated sludge**. A small part of this sludge is pumped back into the aeration tank as an **inoculum**. The rest goes to large tanks called **anaerobic sludge digesters**, where other kinds of bacteria, which grow anaerobically, digest the bacteria and fungi in the sludge. This produces a mixture of gases, **methane, hydrogen sulphide and carbon dioxide**: **biogas**. The treated effluent is released into rivers and streams.

To save rivers from pollution, the **Ministry of Environment and Forests** started the **Ganga Action Plan** and **Yamuna Action Plan** to build large numbers of sewage treatment plants.

**In one breath:** Primary treatment is physical (filtration, sedimentation → primary sludge). Secondary is biological: flocs in aeration tanks cut BOD; activated sludge settles, some returns as inoculum, the rest is digested anaerobically to biogas (CH₄, H₂S, CO₂). BOD = oxygen needed to oxidise organic matter in 1 L of water.

## Microbes in biogas production

**Biogas** is a mixture of gases, mainly **methane**, produced by microbial activity and used as fuel. Bacteria that grow anaerobically on cellulose-rich material and produce large amounts of methane along with CO₂ and H₂ are called **methanogens**; one common genus is ***Methanobacterium***. They are found in the anaerobic sludge of sewage plants and in the **rumen** of cattle, where they help digest cellulose. Cattle dung (**gobar**) is therefore rich in these bacteria and is used to make biogas (gobar gas).

A **biogas plant** has a concrete tank, 10–15 feet deep, into which bio-wastes and a slurry of dung are fed. A floating cover over the slurry rises as gas is produced. The gas goes out through an outlet pipe to homes for cooking and lighting, and the spent slurry is removed through another outlet and used as fertiliser. The technology was developed in India mainly through the efforts of the **Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI)** and the **Khadi and Village Industries Commission (KVIC)**.

**In one breath:** Biogas is mostly methane made by anaerobic methanogens such as *Methanobacterium*, found in cattle rumen and dung. A biogas plant digests dung slurry under a floating cover; spent slurry is manure. IARI and KVIC developed it in India.

## Microbes as biocontrol agents

**Biocontrol** means using biological methods to control plant diseases and pests, instead of chemical pesticides that harm humans, animals and the environment. Organic farmers aim to keep pests at manageable levels by understanding the web of interactions on a farm, rather than eliminating them.

- **Ladybird** beetles and **dragonflies** control **aphids** and **mosquitoes**.
- The bacterium ***Bacillus thuringiensis*** (**Bt**) controls butterfly caterpillars. Dried spores mixed with water are sprayed on plants such as brassicas and fruit trees; caterpillars eat them and the toxin released in their gut kills them, while other insects are unharmed. Its toxin genes have also been introduced into crop plants (see Ch 10).
- The fungus ***Trichoderma*** is a free-living soil fungus that controls several plant pathogens.
- **Baculoviruses** attack insects and other arthropods; most of those used as biocontrol agents belong to the genus ***Nucleopolyhedrovirus***. They are **species-specific, narrow-spectrum** insecticides with no negative effects on plants, mammals, birds, fish or non-target insects, so they are ideal for **integrated pest management (IPM)** and ecologically sensitive areas.

**In one breath:** Ladybirds and dragonflies eat aphids and mosquitoes; Bt kills caterpillars; *Trichoderma* fights plant pathogens; baculoviruses (*Nucleopolyhedrovirus*) are narrow-spectrum insecticides ideal for IPM.

## Microbes as biofertilisers

Chemical fertilisers pollute, so farmers are turning to organic farming and **biofertilisers**: organisms that enrich the nutrient quality of soil. The main sources are bacteria, fungi and cyanobacteria.

- **Rhizobium** lives in the **root nodules of legumes** and fixes atmospheric nitrogen into organic forms the plant can use.
- Free-living soil bacteria such as ***Azospirillum*** and ***Azotobacter*** also fix nitrogen, enriching the soil.
- Fungi form symbiotic associations with plant roots called **mycorrhiza**. Many members of the genus ***Glomus*** form mycorrhiza. The fungus **absorbs phosphorus** from soil and passes it to the plant; such plants also show **resistance to root pathogens**, **tolerance to salinity and drought**, and better overall growth.
- **Cyanobacteria** are autotrophic microbes, widely distributed in water and on land, many of which fix nitrogen: ***Anabaena***, ***Nostoc***, ***Oscillatoria***. In **paddy fields** they are important biofertilisers; blue-green algae also add organic matter to the soil and raise its fertility.

:::tip Free-living vs symbiotic nitrogen fixers
*Rhizobium* is symbiotic (legume root nodules); *Azotobacter* and *Azospirillum* are free-living. *Glomus* is a fungus that supplies phosphorus, not nitrogen.
:::

**In one breath:** Biofertilisers: *Rhizobium* (legume nodules), free-living *Azospirillum* and *Azotobacter* fix nitrogen; mycorrhizal *Glomus* supplies phosphorus and gives disease, salt and drought tolerance; cyanobacteria (*Anabaena*, *Nostoc*, *Oscillatoria*) enrich paddy fields.
