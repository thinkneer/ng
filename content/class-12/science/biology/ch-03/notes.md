# Ch 3 · Reproductive Health

The last chapter of the unit leaves the microscope behind and looks at people and society: how a country keeps its people reproductively healthy, how births can be planned, when pregnancy may be ended legally, how infections spread and how couples who cannot have children can be helped. Marks here come from knowing the exact names of methods and devices, how each one works, and which assisted technique suits which problem.

## What reproductive health means

The World Health Organisation defines **reproductive health** as total well-being in all aspects of reproduction: **physical, emotional, behavioural and social**. So a society is reproductively healthy only when people have working reproductive organs, information, access to care and the freedom to make choices.

India was among the first countries to plan for this at the national level. **Family planning** programmes began in **1951**. Today, broader programmes covering more reproduction-related areas run as **Reproductive and Child Health Care (RCH)** programmes.

How is reproductive health improved? Through awareness using audio-visual and print media; **sex education in schools**, so that young people get correct information and are not misled by myths; information about reproductive organs, adolescence, safe and hygienic sexual practices, sexually transmitted infections and AIDS; teaching couples about birth control, care of pregnant mothers, post-natal care of mother and child, the importance of breastfeeding, and equal opportunities for boys and girls; and medical facilities and support for all reproduction-related problems.

One legal step deserves a separate mention. **Amniocentesis** is a test that finds the sex of a foetus from the chromosomal pattern in the **amniotic fluid** around it. It can detect genetic disorders, but it was being misused to find the sex of the foetus, followed by abortion of girls. So there is a **statutory ban on amniocentesis for sex determination**, to check female foeticide.

Signs that reproductive health has improved include lower **maternal and infant mortality**, more medically assisted deliveries, better post-natal care, more couples with small families, and better detection and cure of sexually transmitted diseases.

**In one breath:** Reproductive health = physical, emotional, behavioural and social well-being in reproduction. India: family planning 1951 → RCH programmes; sex education and awareness; amniocentesis for sex determination is banned.

## Population and birth control

Better health care brought a sharp fall in the death rate, the **maternal mortality rate (MMR)** and the **infant mortality rate (IMR)**, and more people are in the reproductive age. Population therefore rose fast. The world went from about 2 billion people in 1900 to about 6 billion in 2000 and 7.2 billion in 2011. India had about 350 million people at independence, came close to a billion by 2000 and crossed 1.2 billion in May 2011. Even a growth rate below 2% a year (20 per 1000 per year) makes a population grow rapidly.

Measures to slow this include motivating smaller families through contraception, campaigns such as "**Hum Do Hamare Do**" (we two, our two), some young urban couples adopting a one-child norm, raising the legal **marriageable age to 18 for women and 21 for men**, and incentives for couples with small families.

An **ideal contraceptive** should be user-friendly, easily available, effective and reversible, with no or few side effects, and should not interfere with sexual drive, desire or the sexual act.

**In one breath:** Falling death rate, MMR and IMR raised population (India crossed 1.2 billion in 2011). Remedies: contraception, small-family campaigns, marriage age 18/21, incentives. An ideal contraceptive is user-friendly, available, effective, reversible and nearly free of side effects.

## Contraceptive methods

Contraceptive methods fall into six groups. Learn each with its example and how it works, because that is exactly how questions are framed.

**1. Natural methods** aim to stop ovum and sperm from meeting.
- **Periodic abstinence**: avoiding coitus from **day 10 to day 17** of the cycle, when ovulation is expected; this is the **fertile period**.
- **Withdrawal (coitus interruptus)**: the male withdraws before ejaculation.
- **Lactational amenorrhea**: ovulation and the cycle do not occur during intense breastfeeding after delivery, so the chance of conception is almost nil, but only for up to **six months** after parturition.
These have almost no side effects but a **high failure rate**.

**2. Barrier methods** stop sperm from reaching the ovum physically.
- **Condoms** are thin rubber or latex sheaths covering the penis (male) or the vagina and cervix (female). "**Nirodh**" is a popular male condom. Condoms are disposable, self-inserted (so private) and also **protect against STIs and AIDS**, which is why their use has grown.
- **Diaphragms, cervical caps and vaults** are rubber barriers inserted into the female tract to **cover the cervix**. They are **reusable**.
- **Spermicidal** creams, jellies and foams are used with these barriers to raise their efficiency.

**3. Intra-uterine devices (IUDs)** are inserted into the uterus through the vagina by a doctor or trained nurse.
- **Non-medicated IUDs**: Lippes loop.
- **Copper-releasing IUDs**: CuT, Cu7, Multiload 375.
- **Hormone-releasing IUDs**: Progestasert, LNG-20.

IUDs increase **phagocytosis of sperms** in the uterus, and the **copper ions** released **suppress sperm motility and fertilising capacity**. Hormone-releasing IUDs also make the **uterus unsuitable for implantation** and the **cervix hostile to sperms**. IUDs are ideal for women who want to **delay pregnancy or space children**, and are among the most widely accepted methods in India.

:::tip Lippes loop is not copper
Lippes loop is the non-medicated IUD. CuT, Cu7 and Multiload 375 release copper; Progestasert and LNG-20 release hormones.
:::

**4. Oral contraceptives (pills)** contain small doses of **progestogens** or **progestogen–estrogen** combinations. They are taken daily for **21 days**, starting within the first five days of the cycle, then a gap of **7 days** (when menstruation occurs), and the pattern is repeated. Pills **inhibit ovulation and implantation** and alter the cervical mucus to stop or slow sperm entry. They are very effective, have few side effects and are well accepted. **Saheli**, developed at the Central Drug Research Institute (CDRI), Lucknow, is a **non-steroidal**, **once-a-week** pill with very few side effects.

**5. Injections and implants** under the skin contain progestogens alone or with estrogen. They act like pills but work for much longer. **Emergency contraception**: progestogens, progestogen–estrogen combinations or IUDs used within **72 hours** of coitus can prevent pregnancy after rape or unprotected intercourse.

**6. Surgical methods (sterilisation)** are **terminal methods** for those who want no more children. In **vasectomy**, a small part of the **vas deferens** is removed or tied through a small cut in the scrotum. In **tubectomy**, a small part of the **fallopian tube** is removed or tied through a small cut in the abdomen or through the vagina. Both block gamete transport and are highly effective, but their **reversibility is very poor**.

| Method | Examples | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| Natural | periodic abstinence, withdrawal, lactational amenorrhea | avoid ovum–sperm meeting |
| Barrier | condoms, diaphragm, cervical cap, vault | block sperm entry |
| IUD | Lippes loop; CuT, Cu7, Multiload 375; Progestasert, LNG-20 | phagocytosis of sperms; Cu ions; hostile uterus and cervix |
| Pills | progestogen ± estrogen; Saheli | stop ovulation and implantation; change cervical mucus |
| Injections, implants | progestogen ± estrogen | as pills, longer |
| Surgical | vasectomy, tubectomy | block gamete transport |

These methods should not be used without a doctor's advice; possible side effects include nausea, abdominal pain, breakthrough bleeding, irregular menstrual bleeding and even breast cancer.

**In one breath:** Natural (days 10–17, withdrawal, lactational amenorrhea up to 6 months), barriers (Nirodh; diaphragms cover the cervix), IUDs (Lippes loop; Cu-releasing; hormone-releasing), pills (21 days + 7 off; Saheli weekly, non-steroidal), injections/implants, emergency within 72 h, and terminal sterilisation (vasectomy, tubectomy).

## Medical termination of pregnancy

**Medical termination of pregnancy (MTP)**, or induced abortion, is the intentional ending of a pregnancy before full term. Worldwide about 45–50 million MTPs are done each year, about one-fifth of all pregnancies. MTP is not meant for population control, but it does reduce numbers.

India **legalised MTP in 1971** with strict conditions to prevent misuse. As NCERT describes the law (the **MTP (Amendment) Act, 2017**), a pregnancy may be ended on certain grounds within the **first 12 weeks** on the opinion of **one** registered medical practitioner; between **12 and 24 weeks**, **two** registered medical practitioners must agree that the grounds exist. The grounds are that continuing the pregnancy would risk the woman's life or cause grave injury to her physical or mental health, or that there is a substantial risk the child would be born with serious physical or mental abnormalities.

MTPs are done to end unwanted pregnancies (after unprotected intercourse, contraceptive failure or rape) and when continuing a pregnancy would harm the mother, the foetus or both. They are relatively **safe in the first trimester** (up to 12 weeks); second-trimester abortions are much riskier. Two worrying trends are illegal MTPs by unqualified quacks, which are unsafe and can be fatal, and the misuse of amniocentesis to find the sex of the foetus followed by abortion of girls.

:::tip Which law to write
Write the NCERT version: 12 weeks with one registered medical practitioner, 12–24 weeks with two.
:::

**In one breath:** MTP = intentional ending of pregnancy; legal in India since 1971; NCERT: up to 12 weeks with one RMP, 12–24 weeks with two; safest in the first trimester; quacks and sex-selective abortion are the dangers.

## Sexually transmitted infections

Infections spread by sexual contact are **sexually transmitted infections (STIs)**, also called venereal diseases (VD) or reproductive tract infections (RTI). Common ones are **gonorrhoea, syphilis, genital herpes, chlamydiasis, genital warts, trichomoniasis, hepatitis-B and HIV** (which leads to AIDS).

Some, like hepatitis-B and HIV, also spread by sharing injection needles or surgical instruments, by transfusion of infected blood, and from an infected mother to her foetus. Except for **hepatitis-B, genital herpes and HIV**, the others are completely curable if detected early and treated properly.

Early symptoms are mild: itching, fluid discharge, slight pain and swelling in the genital region. Infected women may have no symptoms at all for a long time. Mild symptoms and social stigma keep people from getting tested and treated, and that is dangerous, because untreated STIs can lead to **pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)**, abortions, still births, **ectopic pregnancy**, infertility and even cancer of the reproductive tract. Anyone can be infected, but cases are highest in the **15–24 years** age group.

Prevention comes down to three rules: avoid sex with unknown or multiple partners; always use condoms during coitus; and, in case of doubt, see a qualified doctor early and complete the treatment.

**In one breath:** STIs include gonorrhoea, syphilis, genital herpes, chlamydiasis, genital warts, trichomoniasis, hepatitis-B and HIV; hepatitis-B, herpes and HIV are not fully curable. Untreated STIs cause PID, ectopic pregnancy and infertility. Prevent: no unknown or multiple partners, condoms, early treatment.

## Infertility and assisted reproductive technologies

Many couples are **infertile**: they cannot have children despite unprotected sexual co-habitation. Causes may be physical, congenital, disease-related, drug-related, immunological or psychological. In India the woman is often blamed, but the problem is just as often with the man. Specialised clinics can diagnose and treat some causes; when correction is impossible, **assisted reproductive technologies (ART)** can help.

**In vitro fertilisation followed by embryo transfer (IVF–ET)**, the "test-tube baby" programme, works like this. Ova from the wife or a donor and sperms from the husband or a donor are collected and made to form a zygote in the laboratory, under conditions like those in the body. The zygote or early embryo is then transferred:

- **ZIFT** (zygote intra-fallopian transfer): zygote or early embryo with **up to 8 blastomeres** into the **fallopian tube**.
- **IUT** (intra-uterine transfer): embryo with **more than 8 blastomeres** into the **uterus**.

Embryos formed by fertilisation inside a woman's body (in vivo) can also be transferred to help women who cannot conceive. Other techniques:

- **GIFT** (gamete intra-fallopian transfer): an **ovum from a donor** is placed in the fallopian tube of a woman who **cannot produce ova** but can provide a suitable environment for fertilisation and development.
- **ICSI** (intra-cytoplasmic sperm injection): a **sperm is injected directly into the ovum** in the laboratory to form an embryo.
- **Artificial insemination (AI)**: for men who cannot inseminate or have **very low sperm counts**, semen from the husband or a healthy donor is placed in the **vagina**, or in the **uterus** (**IUI**, intra-uterine insemination).

| Technique | What is transferred, where | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| ZIFT | zygote / embryo (≤ 8 cells) → fallopian tube | woman who cannot conceive |
| IUT | embryo (> 8 cells) → uterus | woman who cannot conceive |
| GIFT | donor ovum → fallopian tube | woman who cannot produce ova |
| ICSI | sperm injected into ovum (lab) | very low sperm count or motility |
| AI / IUI | semen → vagina / uterus | man cannot inseminate; low sperm count |

These techniques need highly skilled professionals and costly equipment, so they are available in only a few centres and only some can afford them; emotional, religious and social factors also hold people back. With so many orphaned and destitute children in India, **legal adoption** remains one of the best options for couples who want to be parents.

:::tip ZIFT vs GIFT
ZIFT transfers a zygote (fertilisation already done in the lab); GIFT transfers a gamete, the donor ovum, so fertilisation happens inside the body.
:::

**In one breath:** Infertility has many causes and can lie with either partner. IVF–ET → ZIFT (≤ 8 cells, tube) or IUT (> 8 cells, uterus); GIFT puts a donor ovum in the tube; ICSI injects a sperm into the ovum; AI/IUI places semen in the vagina or uterus. ART is costly and rare, so adoption is a strong option.
