# Ch 1 · Understanding Social Science

In Classes 6 to 8 you met Social Science as stories about people, places and events. Class 9 asks a bigger question: **what is this subject, and why does it matter?** This short opening chapter answers that. It gives you the vocabulary the whole book uses (disciplines, sources, evidence), and school papers like to test it with one-liners, "differentiate" questions and a short source-based case. Read it once properly; it makes every later chapter easier.

## What Social Science studies

Humans live in societies. We depend on each other for food, shelter, safety and ideas, and our lives are shaped by four big things: the **environment** around us, the **institutions** that govern us, the **economic activity** that feeds and employs us, and the **traditions and ideas** handed down over generations.

**Social Science is the systematic study of human society.** "Systematic" is the key word: it does not collect random facts. It asks questions, gathers evidence and reasons from it. And it goes beyond *what happened* and *where things are* to ask **why** things happen, **how** people live together, how environments affect life, how governments and economies work, and how the past shapes the present.

How is that different from Physics, Chemistry and Biology? Those are **natural sciences**: they study the natural world (matter, energy, living things). Social Science studies **society**: institutions, cultures and the way humans interact, and how all of these connect.

**In one breath:** Social Science is the systematic study of human society. It explains why and how, not just what and where, and it studies society the way natural sciences study nature.

## Social Science in a normal day

Think about one day in your life. The book's simple trick is to follow ordinary things back to the people and systems behind them.

- **Your house** is built from materials (cement, steel, bricks, wood) that came from different places, made and moved by many workers.
- **Your food** passed through many hands before it reached your plate: it was grown, harvested, processed, transported, bought and sold.
- **The road** outside was planned, paid for and repaired by public authorities.
- **Your school** runs on education policies and programmes decided by governments.
- **Electricity** reaches your switch from a distant power station through a huge network.

Every one of these is a social science question. Who made the decision? Where did it come from? Who paid, who earned? What changed over time?

## One reality, four lenses

Society is too complex for a single subject, so Social Science is a **group of disciplines**. Each looks at the *same* reality from its own angle. In Classes 9 and 10 you study four core ones.

- **Geography** studies places, people and things, how they are spread over the Earth, and how humans and the natural environment affect each other.
- **History** studies the human past and how things changed over time, using many kinds of sources.
- **Political Science** studies power and decision-making: how governments work, constitutions, laws, policies, social movements and nation-building.
- **Economics** studies how societies use **limited resources** to meet needs: production, exchange and distribution, and also well-being and fairness.

The wider family includes **Sociology, Anthropology, Psychology and Philosophy**, which you will meet in higher classes.

The book's favourite example is a **drought**. One event touches all four lenses at once.

## How social scientists find out

A social scientist cannot put society in a test tube, so the work runs on **evidence**. Each discipline has its own toolkit.

**Geography** uses **maps, globes and atlases**, **GIS** (Geographic Information Systems, computer maps built in layers such as roads, rivers and population), **infographics**, and fieldwork (observing a place directly).

**History** works from **sources**:

## Roots in Indian knowledge traditions

Careful thinking about society is old in India. The book picks out a few ideas that show observation, questioning and reasoning long before "social science" was a school subject.

- **Panchamahābhūtas**, the five great elements: earth, water, fire, air and space. Seeing nature as five connected elements is an early **systems view** of the environment.
- **Vasudhaiva Kuṭumbakam**, "the world is one family": societies are interconnected, an idea that fits today's globalised world.
- **Arthaśāstra**, attributed to **Kauṭilya** and composed about **2,300 years ago**: a treatise on administration, taxation, the economy and the duties of rulers. It is one of the earliest systematic works on government and economics.
- The **Itihāsa–Purāṇa** tradition: stories that preserved cultural memory and historical information.
- Ideas of **dharma** (duty, right conduct), **artha** (material well-being) and **rājadharma** (the duty of a ruler towards the people) linked governance with ethics. Today, **Panchayati Raj** continues the tradition of local self-government, with citizens taking part in village decisions.

**In one breath:** Indian traditions show early systematic thinking about society: the Panchamahābhūtas (a systems view of nature), Vasudhaiva Kuṭumbakam (one interconnected world), Kauṭilya's Arthaśāstra (government and economy) and the Itihāsa–Purāṇa (memory of the past).

## Why study Social Science?

Four reasons, each worth a point in a long answer:

1. **To understand everyday systems.** Prices, laws, elections, maps and news make sense only if you know how they work.
2. **To build empathy.** India is hugely diverse; Social Science helps you understand people whose lives are different from yours.
3. **To be an informed citizen in a democracy.** Voting, questioning authority and taking part in local decisions all need knowledge.
4. **To connect past, present and future.** Choices made in the past shape today, and today's choices shape tomorrow.

The big problems of your lifetime (climate change, migration, new technology, global connections) are social science problems. Solving them needs the same habits this subject trains: **observe, ask questions, and reason from evidence**. Society does not run by chance; there are patterns, and evidence helps us explain them.

**In one breath:** We study Social Science to understand everyday systems, respect diversity, act as informed citizens and see how the past shapes the future. Its core habit is observing, questioning and reasoning from evidence.
